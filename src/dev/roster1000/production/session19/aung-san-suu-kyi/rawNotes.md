## Life Period 1 — Childhood and Family (1945–1960)

- Born 19 June 1945 in Rangoon, British Burma, to Aung San (founder of the
  modern Burmese army and the chief negotiator of Burma's independence from
  the United Kingdom) and Khin Kyi. [WIKI]
- Father assassinated in 1947 by political rivals, when Aung San Suu Kyi was
  two years old — she has no direct memory of him and was raised almost
  entirely on his reputation and the accounts of others. [WIKI, TIME]
- Her younger brother, Aung San Lin, died at age eight by drowning in an
  ornamental lake at the family home; her surviving brother, Aung San Oo,
  later emigrated to the United States (San Diego). [WIKI]
- Mother, Khin Kyi, became a prominent public figure in her own right,
  appointed Burmese ambassador to India and Nepal in 1960 — meaning Aung San
  Suu Kyi's adolescence was shaped by a second high-profile, politically
  engaged parent, not only by her father's posthumous legacy. [WIKI]
- Attended a Methodist English high school in Rangoon as a child, noted for
  facility with languages. [WIKI]

## Life Period 2 — Education Abroad: India, Oxford, New York (1960–1972)

- 1960: moved to India with her mother following the ambassadorial
  appointment; attended the Convent of Jesus and Mary School in New Delhi,
  then Lady Shri Ram College (University of Delhi), graduating in 1964 with
  a degree in politics. [WIKI]
- 1964–67: attended St Hugh's College, Oxford, earning a BA in Philosophy,
  Politics and Economics with third-class honours (later upgraded to an MA
  per Oxford convention in 1968) — a specifically unexceptional undergraduate
  result, not a mark of early academic distinction. [WIKI]
- 1968–71: worked for roughly three years at the United Nations in New York,
  principally on budget-related administrative matters — not a high-profile
  or political posting. [WIKI] Lived during this period with a family
  friend, the former Burmese singer Ma Than E.
- Later held a two-year research fellowship at the Indian Institute of
  Advanced Study in Shimla, India, and did government-related research work
  connected to the Union of Burma. [WIKI]
- 1 January 1972: married Michael Aris, a British scholar of Tibetan and
  Himalayan culture then based in Bhutan, whom she had met at Oxford. [WIKI,
  TIME]
- Sons: Alexander Aris born 1973 in London; Kim Aris born 1977. [WIKI]
- 1985–87: pursued graduate research toward a Master of Philosophy in
  Burmese literature at the School of Oriental and African Studies (SOAS),
  University of London — a specifically scholarly, literature-focused
  pursuit in the years immediately before her political life began, with no
  indication at the time that she was preparing for a political role.
  [WIKI]
- By her own later account in "Freedom from Fear," she describes her father
  Aung San's own character at length — his willingness "to speak the truth,
  to stand by his word, to accept criticism, to admit his faults, to correct
  his mistakes, to respect the opposition, to parley with the enemy and to
  let people be the judge of his worthiness as a leader" — explicitly framing
  this description of her father's courage as the model she holds up for
  people confronting fear in Burma. [OWN-WRITING]

## Life Period 3 — Return to Burma, the 8888 Uprising, and the NLD's Founding (March–December 1988)

- Returned to Burma in March 1988, prompted by her mother Khin Kyi's stroke,
  intending to nurse her — not with any stated political intention at the
  time of return. [WIKI, TIME, POPHAM]
- Within months, Burma's long-serving military ruler Ne Win stepped down
  (1988) amid the "8888 Uprising" — mass student- and civilian-led
  pro-democracy demonstrations that began 8 August 1988 and were violently
  suppressed by the military. [WIKI]
- 24 August 1988: made her first public appearance connected to the
  uprising, addressing protesters gathered at Yangon General Hospital. [WIKI]
- 26 August 1988: addressed a mass rally of an estimated half a million
  people at the Shwedagon Pagoda, Rangoon's most sacred Buddhist site,
  publicly calling for a democratic government for the first time. In this
  speech she directly and explicitly grounded her entry into public politics
  in her father's legacy: "I could not as my father's daughter remain
  indifferent to all that was going on." [WIKI, SEARCH-AGG, OWN-WRITING] —
  from this point she rapidly assumed a leading role in the pro-democracy
  movement, moving from private citizen to mass-rally speaker within roughly
  six months of returning to the country.
- September 1988: a new military junta, the State Peace and Development
  Council (later State Law and Order Restoration Council), seized power amid
  the unrest.
- 27 September 1988: co-founded the National League for Democracy (NLD)
  alongside U Aung Gyi (a former senior military officer, who became
  president), U Tin Oo (also a former senior military officer, vice
  president), and others; Aung San Suu Kyi herself took the post of general
  secretary — i.e., the founding leadership of Burma's main pro-democracy
  party was built substantially from retired military figures critical of
  the junta, not solely civilian dissidents. [SEARCH-AGG, WIKI]
- 3 December 1988: Aung Gyi resigned from the NLD, publicly alleging
  communist infiltration of the party — an early, documented internal
  leadership rupture within months of the party's founding. [SEARCH-AGG]
- October–December 1988: undertook a nationwide speech-making tour to large
  audiences, in defiance of a government ban on political gatherings.
  [SEARCH-AGG]
- Her stated commitment to nonviolent methods during this period was later
  clarified, in her own words from a 2007 statement, as resting on
  "political and practical reasons rather than moral grounds" — i.e., she
  has documented her own nonviolent strategy as a calculated political
  choice as much as (or more than) an ethical absolute. [WIKI]

## Life Period 4 — The 1989 Danubyu Standoff, First Arrest, and Hunger Strike (1989)

- April 1989: during a campaign procession through the delta town of
  Danubyu, soldiers under the command of an army captain knelt and took aim
  with rifles at Aung San Suu Kyi and her group; the captain had received
  written orders to open fire. Documented accounts describe her motioning
  her followers to move to the side of the road while she herself continued
  walking directly toward the levelled guns; the order to fire was
  countermanded before she reached the soldiers, and she walked through the
  line. [SEARCH-AGG] — a single, discrete, high-stakes, personally
  dangerous episode of continuing forward under direct, individually-aimed
  lethal threat rather than retreating or negotiating from a distance.
- 20 July 1989: placed under house arrest for the first time, at her
  mother's residence on University Avenue, Rangoon, under Burma's 1975 State
  Protection Act (martial-law provisions allowing detention without charge
  or trial for up to three years, later repeatedly extended via additional
  legal instruments). [HRW, WIKI]
- Shortly after this detention began, soldiers raided the NLD compound and
  seized roughly 40 party members, delivering them to Insein Prison. In
  response, Aung San Suu Kyi undertook a hunger strike specifically to
  demand humane treatment for the detained members and reportedly asked to
  be imprisoned herself alongside the arrested students. She lost
  approximately twelve pounds over twelve days and required intravenous
  nourishment; she ended the strike once the junta (State Law and Order
  Restoration Council) promised fair treatment of the detained members.
  [SEARCH-AGG] — a documented instance of risking her own physical health
  through self-imposed deprivation as a direct-action lever specifically on
  behalf of subordinate party members' treatment, rather than for her own
  release or any personal demand.
- She was offered release on the condition that she leave the country; she
  refused this offer, a pattern that recurred at multiple later points in
  her detention. [WIKI, HRW]

## Life Period 5 — First Detention, the 1990 Election, and International Recognition (1989–1995)

- 27 May 1990: the NLD won a landslide victory in Burma's first
  multi-party general election since 1960, taking approximately 80-82% of
  parliamentary seats (roughly 59% of the popular vote) — while Aung San
  Suu Kyi herself remained under house arrest and unable to campaign in
  person during the vote itself. [WIKI, HRW]
- The ruling military junta refused to recognize the election result or
  transfer power, nullifying the outcome outright, which produced sustained
  international condemnation. [WIKI]
- During this period, party colleagues who had joined the NLD during the
  1988 crisis (including some ex-military figures) left the party,
  reportedly believing her more confrontational approach toward the
  military conflicted with a genuinely nonviolent strategy — i.e., a
  documented instance of her own tactical stance under pressure costing her
  some allied support from within her own coalition, not uniting it. [WIKI]
- 1990: awarded the Sakharov Prize for Freedom of Thought by the European
  Parliament.
- 1991: awarded the Nobel Peace Prize while still under house arrest, "for
  her non-violent struggle for democracy and human rights" (Norwegian Nobel
  Committee's own citation). She was unable to travel to Oslo to accept it
  in person; her sons Alexander and Kim Aris accepted the prize on her
  behalf. [WIKI, HRW] The prize money (US$1.3 million) was used to establish
  a health and education trust for the Burmese people rather than retained
  personally. [WIKI]
- 20 September 1994: met with junta leaders Senior General Than Shwe and
  General Khin Nyunt — her first direct meeting with the ruling generals
  since her detention began five years earlier. [WIKI]
- 10 July 1995: released from her first period of house arrest, after
  roughly six years. [HRW, WIKI]
- 1995: delivered the keynote address (by videotape, since she could not
  travel) to the Fourth World Conference on Women in Beijing.

## Life Period 6 — Release, Renewed Restriction, Violence, and Family Separation (1995–2000)

- December 1995: the last time she and her husband Michael Aris met in
  person, at a point when travel restrictions on him were tightening; from
  this point onward the Burmese government progressively denied him
  re-entry visas. [WIKI]
- Throughout the mid-to-late 1990s, while nominally "free," she continued
  to address supporters gathered at the gate of her house on University
  Avenue on weekends — a sustained, regular public routine even without
  formal detention in force at that specific moment. [TIME]
- 9 November 1996: her motorcade (carrying her along with senior colleagues
  Tin Oo and Kyi Maung) was attacked by roughly 200 men wielding chains,
  batons, and stones, breaking the rear window of her car. The attackers
  were believed to be members of the pro-junta Union Solidarity and
  Development Association, reportedly paid a small sum each to participate.
  A police complaint was filed; no action was taken against the attackers.
  [WIKI]
- 1998: an Italian journalist who photographed her had all film, tapes, and
  notes confiscated by Burmese customs officials — one specific documented
  instance of the state's information-control apparatus operating directly
  against outside coverage of her. [WIKI]
- 1997: Michael Aris was diagnosed with terminal prostate cancer.
- 23 September 2000: placed under house arrest for a second time (having
  been under increasing restriction on her movements since early 1997).
  [HRW]

## Life Period 7 — Michael Aris's Death and the Decision Not to Leave Burma (1997–1999)

- Throughout 1997–99, despite direct appeals to the Burmese government from
  prominent figures including the UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan, the Pope,
  and the government of the United States, the ruling junta consistently
  refused to grant Michael Aris an entry visa to see his wife one final
  time, even once his cancer was terminal. Aris had seen his wife in person
  only five times in the preceding decade. [WIKI, SEARCH-AGG]
- The Burmese government's stated position, conveyed to Aris, was that he
  should instead pursue reunion by having his wife leave the country to
  visit him in the UK. Aung San Suu Kyi chose not to leave Burma to see her
  dying husband, reasoning (per multiple corroborating accounts of her own
  stated position) that the government would not allow her to re-enter the
  country afterward, ending her political role and separating her
  permanently from the movement she led. [WIKI, SEARCH-AGG]
- 27 March 1999: Michael Aris died of cancer in Oxford, on his 53rd
  birthday, without having seen his wife again since 1995. [WIKI] Aung San
  Suu Kyi did not travel to the funeral.
- She has explicitly, publicly framed the cost of this choice in terms of
  motherhood specifically, stating: "As a mother, the greater sacrifice was
  giving up my sons, but I was always aware of the fact that others had
  given up more than me." [WIKI] — a documented instance of her explaining
  a personally costly decision by comparing her own sacrifice to that of
  others (implicitly, imprisoned or killed NLD colleagues) rather than
  describing it as uniquely severe.
- She was separated from her two sons, who remained in the United Kingdom,
  for an extended period; they were not reunited in Burma until 2011. [WIKI]

## Life Period 8 — Second Detention, UN Mediation, and Release (2000–2003)

- 6 May 2002: released after roughly 19 months of house arrest, following
  UN-facilitated confidence-building negotiations between her and the
  junta. Upon release she publicly declared "a new dawn for the country."
  [HRW, WIKI] Government officials framed the release as reflecting that
  "we are confident that we can trust each other."
- UN Special Envoy Razali Ismail, who had brokered contact between her and
  the junta during this period, later resigned his role partly over being
  repeatedly denied re-entry to Burma himself. [WIKI]

## Life Period 9 — The Depayin Massacre and Third Detention (2003–2010)

- 30 May 2003: her motorcade, traveling through Sagaing Region near the
  village of Depayin/Kyee, was ambushed by a mob organized by the
  pro-government Union Solidarity and Development Association and the Swan
  Ah Shin militia, who blocked the road. At least 70 people (accounts range
  upward) associated with her convoy were killed. [HRW, SEARCH-AGG] A later
  (2015) account by Khin Nyunt, the former junta Secretary-1, stated that
  the ruling council's chairman had directly ordered the convoy stopped "by
  all means." [SEARCH-AGG]
- Aung San Suu Kyi and her driver escaped the immediate ambush, but she was
  arrested shortly afterward at Ye-U/Ye-Oo along with senior colleague Tin
  Oo. [HRW, SEARCH-AGG]
- She was held in secret detention at Insein Prison for more than three
  months following the attack. [WIKI] During this period she underwent a
  hysterectomy (September 2003), after which she was moved to house arrest
  at her Rangoon residence rather than continued prison detention. [WIKI]
- 25 May 2007: her house arrest was extended for a further year, despite a
  direct personal appeal from UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan to junta
  leader Than Shwe. [WIKI]
- 22 September 2007: during the "Saffron Revolution" (mass Buddhist-monk-led
  protests sparked by fuel price increases), she made a brief, unscheduled
  public appearance at the gate of her residence — still under house arrest
  — to receive blessings from a column of monks marching in support of
  human rights; she was reportedly moved to Insein Prison the following day
  for a period, though UN envoy meetings in late September/early October
  indicate she remained under house-arrest-type restriction. [WIKI]
- 27 May 2008: house arrest extended for a further year — an extension
  multiple international legal observers characterized as itself illegal
  under both international and Burmese domestic law at the time. [WIKI]
- 3 May 2009: an American citizen, John Yettaw, swam uninvited across Inya
  Lake to her residence and stayed two nights, claiming a "divine vision"
  had warned him of an assassination plot against her (he had made a
  similar, unsuccessful attempt two years earlier). She allowed him to stay
  briefly before he departed by swimming back. [WIKI, TIME]
- 13 May 2009: arrested for violating the terms of her house arrest as a
  result of the Yettaw incident (regardless of her own lack of control over
  his uninvited entry), facing a possible five-year sentence; taken to
  Insein Prison for trial. [WIKI, HRW]
- The trial (beginning 18 May 2009) was documented as procedurally
  one-sided: the defense was permitted only one of four proposed witnesses
  while the prosecution called fourteen; the defense's proposed NLD
  character witnesses (Tin Oo, Win Tin) were rejected by the court; foreign
  diplomats and journalists were initially barred from proceedings. [WIKI]
- She spent her 64th birthday (19 June 2009) in detention, sharing biryani
  rice and a chocolate cake with her guards — a specific, small, documented
  human detail of her conduct even within an adversarial custodial
  relationship. [WIKI]
- 11 August 2009: convicted and sentenced to three years with hard labor;
  the sentence was immediately commuted by the junta to 18 months of
  further house arrest — a sentence whose timing effectively prevented her
  participation in Burma's scheduled 2010 general election. [HRW, WIKI]
  International condemnation followed from the UN Secretary-General, the UN
  Security Council, Western governments, and even some ASEAN member states.
- Throughout her multiple detention periods overall (1989–2010, roughly 15
  of 21 years), documented daily conduct included: rising at 4:30 a.m. for
  meditation; extensive reading (philosophy, Buddhism, biographies of
  Gandhi, Nehru, and Mandela specifically, and reportedly the Encyclopedia
  Britannica); household chores while listening to BBC World Service
  broadcasts on radio for outside news; and, in the earlier part of her
  detention, regular piano practice (reportedly Mozart), which she stopped
  once Burma's humidity warped the instrument and put it out of tune. She
  lived without a telephone, computer, or internet access for most of this
  period, with only patchy electricity. [TIME, SEARCH-AGG]
- 13 November 2010: released from house arrest, six days after a
  internationally-criticized general election from which she had been
  barred from participating. Analysts noted the timing coincided with the
  scheduled expiry of her 2009 sentence, and some (e.g., New York Times
  coverage cited via search) suggested the military judged itself confident
  enough in its post-election position to permit her release. A large crowd
  of supporters gathered at her house once security barricades were
  removed. [WIKI]

## Life Period 10 — Return to Public Politics, Parliament, and the 2015 Landslide (2010–2016)

- November 2010: her son Kim Aris was granted a visa to visit her — his
  first visit to Burma in ten years. [WIKI]
- July and August 2011: made her first trips outside Yangon since 2003 (to
  Bagan, then Pegu), both accompanied by her son.
- Late 2011: engaged directly with the reforming government, including a
  telephone conference with US President Barack Obama and a December 2011
  in-person meeting with US Secretary of State Hillary Clinton in Yangon —
  a documented, deliberate pivot toward direct diplomatic engagement with a
  government she had spent decades opposing, once genuine reform signals
  appeared. [WIKI]
- 2012: stood as a parliamentary candidate in the Kawhmu constituency
  by-elections; the NLD won 43 of 45 contested seats, and she became a
  member of the Pyithu Hluttaw (House of Representatives). [WIKI]
- November 2015: the NLD won a landslide general election victory (roughly
  86% of contested seats in the combined legislature) — the first
  broadly credible nationwide election since 1990. [WIKI]
- She was constitutionally barred from serving as President herself, under
  a clause (widely understood to have been drafted specifically to exclude
  her) prohibiting the presidency to anyone whose spouse or children hold
  foreign citizenship — her late husband and both sons were British
  citizens. [WIKI]
- In response, the newly created post of "State Counsellor" (a
  prime-minister-analogous role) was established for her by her own party's
  legislative majority, alongside her simultaneously holding the Union
  Minister of Foreign Affairs portfolio; she was widely described in
  international coverage as Myanmar's de facto civilian leader from this
  point despite not holding the presidency itself. [WIKI]
- Documented instance of direct party discipline: in November 2015, she is
  reported to have "laid down the law" on party discipline to newly elected
  NLD members of parliament, a specific instance of asserting strict
  top-down control immediately upon her party's electoral victory rather
  than a looser, more collegial post-election transition. [SEARCH-AGG]

## Life Period 11 — Governance as State Counsellor: Documented Leadership Pattern (2016–2021)

- Multiple independent, named critical assessments converge on the same
  specific leadership pattern during this period: colleagues described her
  management approach as running "her party like a dictator" who "doesn't
  listen, or accept advice"; a former US Ambassador (Derek Mitchell)
  publicly identified "limited communication with the media, tense
  relations with civil society, a top-down style of leadership, gridlock in
  decision-making." [AJ-TROUBLE]
- A separate scholarly assessment (Journal of Democracy) documents that
  even routine legislative business required NLD parliamentarians to
  "clear their questions from the floor with party leaders" before raising
  them — a specific, granular instance of centralized approval bottlenecking
  ordinary government business, not merely a general impression of
  centralization. [JOD]
- Documented personnel decision: her government's Finance Minister, Kyaw
  Win, held a degree from an unaccredited institution ("Brooklyn Park
  University," widely characterized as a diploma mill); her Commerce
  Minister, Than Myint, held credentials from a similarly unaccredited,
  closed correspondence school. Despite public ridicule over these
  appointments, she did not replace either minister. [JOD] — documented
  instance of persisting with an initial personnel choice under public
  criticism rather than reversing it.
- Documented instance of favoring personal control over institution-building:
  she made "no known arrangements for a successor" despite being in her
  early seventies during this period and dominating decision-making
  personally at every level of her own party. [JOD]
- The NLD under her leadership enforced what is characterized as "Leninist"
  party discipline — internal questioning of government positions was
  actively discouraged, and a party member who publicly criticized the
  military's continued political role received no public defense from the
  party leadership. [JOD]
- Documented instance in ethnic-minority-area electoral strategy: the NLD
  ran its own candidates in ethnic minority regions without consulting
  local ethnic party leaders, and — even where local ethnic parties had won
  the popular vote in specific areas — used constitutional provisions to
  install NLD-appointed chief ministers in those regions regardless. [JOD]
- Documented instance of a symbolically costly, avoidable choice: the
  government insisted on naming a bridge in Mon State after her father
  Aung San (who already had thousands of existing namesakes/memorials)
  despite local public demonstrations against the choice; the NLD's
  subsequent by-election performance in the area fell to roughly half its
  prior support, losing the specific seat to a military-backed candidate.
  [JOD]
- Documented instance of a self-inflicted strategic sequencing error: she
  publicly declared ethnic peace the "national priority" and explicitly
  linked progress on it to constitutional amendments — even though the
  military retained a constitutionally guaranteed one-quarter bloc of
  parliamentary seats sufficient to veto any such amendment outright,
  making the linkage foreseeably self-defeating. Multiple subsequent peace
  conferences under her leadership produced no new signatories to the
  existing ceasefire agreement, and conflict intensified rather than eased
  in Rakhine and Shan States during this period. [JOD]
- October 2016: personally requested the lifting of most remaining US
  economic sanctions on Myanmar — removing a significant piece of external
  leverage over the military leadership at a point before the Rohingya
  crisis had come to a head. [JOD]
- Under her government, the existing criminal-defamation statute
  (Telecommunications Law, Section 66D) was used to imprison dozens of
  people who criticized the government or her personally; her party held a
  sufficient parliamentary majority to reform or repeal the law but did not
  do so. [AJ-TROUBLE, JOD]
- Documented instance of a specific dismissive government response during
  the Rakhine crisis: an official government Facebook page under her
  administration dismissed a Rohingya woman's sexual-assault allegation
  against security forces as a "fake rape" story. [JOD]
- Separately, on economic governance: her government did not issue a
  coherent economic policy statement until roughly four months after taking
  office (July 2016); foreign direct investment fell from approximately
  $9.5 billion to $6.9 billion during her government's first year; she did
  not prioritize rural electrification (despite roughly two-thirds of the
  country lacking electricity access) until August 2017; and specific,
  well-documented regulatory friction (e.g., sixteen separate licenses
  required for a construction permit) went unaddressed during this period.
  [JOD]
- Not every relationship in this period was adversarial or centralizing:
  she is documented as having engaged directly and repeatedly with foreign
  governments and figures (Obama, Clinton) once she judged genuine reform
  possible, and the NLD's institutional core (its senior decision-making
  body) is described as an aging but stable and loyal group around her,
  even if criticized externally for its lack of generational turnover.
  [WIKI, JOD]

## Life Period 12 — The Rohingya Crisis, Silence, and International Fallout (2016–2019)

- From August 2017 onward — following documented Myanmar military
  operations against Rohingya communities in Rakhine State that
  international bodies and journalists characterized as involving mass
  killing, rape, and arson, precipitating a large-scale refugee exodus to
  Bangladesh — she maintained public silence on the specific military
  conduct for an extended period, and avoided interviews with domestic
  Myanmar journalists throughout, restricting her public communication
  largely to occasional appearances before foreign media. [AJ-TROUBLE, JOD]
- She is documented as having refused to publicly acknowledge that the
  Tatmadaw (Myanmar's armed forces) had committed the atrocities alleged by
  international investigators and human rights organizations. [WIKI]
- Her government prosecuted journalists covering the crisis, and restricted
  independent press access to the conflict zone. [WIKI, JOD]
- During the 2015 general-election campaign — before the crisis escalated
  to its most severe documented phase — she is reported to have largely
  avoided discussing the Rohingya issue at all, a choice attributed by
  observers to an awareness that a more sympathetic public stance would
  cost electoral support among Myanmar's Buddhist-majority electorate.
  [JOD]

## Life Period 13 — The Hague: Defending Myanmar at the ICJ (December 2019)

- 11 November 2019: The Gambia filed a case against Myanmar at the
  International Court of Justice alleging violations of the UN Genocide
  Convention in Myanmar's treatment of the Rohingya.
- 10–12 December 2019: Aung San Suu Kyi, in her capacity as State
  Counsellor and Myanmar's de facto head of government, personally led
  Myanmar's legal delegation to The Hague and delivered a roughly
  30-minute speech to the court in Myanmar's own defense — a documented,
  deliberate personal choice to appear and argue the case herself rather
  than delegate it entirely to lawyers or subordinate officials. [AJ-ICJ,
  multiple corroborating sources]
- In her own words at the hearing, she directly challenged the genocide
  characterization, asking the court: "How can there be an ongoing
  genocide or genocidal intent when these concrete steps are being taken in
  Rakhine?" — citing development initiatives, a citizenship-verification
  program, and the closure of some displaced-persons camps as counter-
  evidence. [AJ-ICJ]
- She framed the 2017 violence as a domestic counter-insurgency response
  to a specific, named armed group (the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army,
  which she said had launched coordinated attacks on more than 30 police
  posts on 25 August 2017), rather than as a campaign directed at
  civilians as such. [AJ-ICJ]
- She argued that any individual military misconduct should be addressed
  through Myanmar's own domestic courts-martial process rather than
  international adjudication, explicitly invoking the international-law
  principle of complementarity (that international courts should defer to
  functioning domestic accountability mechanisms). [AJ-ICJ]
- She distinguished, in her own legal argument, between the possibility of
  disproportionate use of force or inadequate civilian protection (which she
  did not entirely rule out as a legal matter) and the specific, much
  higher legal bar of genocidal intent (which she flatly denied). [AJ-ICJ]
- Her delivery was documented by observers as measured and controlled in
  tone throughout. [SEARCH-AGG]
- Her appearance and defense of Myanmar's military drew a sharp public
  reaction outside the courtroom: Rohingya supporters gathered outside the
  ICJ carrying a "Stop Genocide" banner and photographs of her captioned
  "Shame" and "agent of the military." Commentary at the time explicitly
  framed her appearance as a "remarkable recalibration" of her global
  reputation — from an internationally idolized former political prisoner
  to a sitting head of government personally defending the same military
  that had once imprisoned her, now accused of mass atrocities against a
  different, less-powerful minority population. [AJ-ICJ, multiple
  corroborating sources]
- The ICJ subsequently (January 2020) ordered Myanmar to take provisional
  measures to protect the Rohingya population pending the case's full
  resolution; her own government publicly continued to deny that genocide
  had occurred. [SEARCH-AGG]

## Life Period 14 — The November 2020 Election and the February 2021 Coup (2020–2021)

- November 2020: the NLD won Myanmar's general election again, by another
  large margin. [WIKI]
- 1 February 2021: the Myanmar military (Tatmadaw) seized power in a coup
  d'état, rejecting the November 2020 election results as fraudulent (a
  claim independent international election observers did not support);
  Aung San Suu Kyi and President Win Myint were both arrested on the same
  day, before the new parliament could be seated. [WIKI, SEARCH-AGG]
- Initial charges filed against her cited walkie-talkie radios found during
  a search of her residence, alleged to have been imported and used without
  proper authorization — widely characterized by independent legal
  observers as a pretextual charge designed to provide legal cover for what
  was, in substance, a military seizure of power. [SEARCH-AGG]
- Between mid-February and early April 2021, at least five additional
  charges were filed against her, including a charge under Myanmar's
  National Disaster Law tied to her conduct during the COVID-19 pandemic
  period. [SEARCH-AGG]
- The coup triggered sustained nationwide protest and a broad civil
  disobedience movement; independent monitoring groups documented
  approximately 850 civilian deaths in the military's suppression of these
  protests in the following months. [SEARCH-AGG]
- Her subsequent trials (through 2021–2022) proceeded under conditions
  widely condemned as procedurally compromised by human rights
  organizations — Human Rights Watch characterized the outcome as
  effectively "guaranteed" from the moment of her February 2021 detention.
  [SEARCH-AGG] She was convicted across a series of separate proceedings
  (December 2021, January 2022, October 2022, December 2022) on charges
  including incitement, breach of COVID-19 restrictions, illegal
  importation/possession of communications equipment, and corruption,
  accumulating an eventual sentence total of 33 years, later administratively
  reduced to 27. [WIKI, SEARCH-AGG]
- She continued to serve nominally as NLD general secretary during this
  period of imprisonment, and as party chairperson until the NLD itself was
  formally dissolved/deregistered by the junta in 2023. [WIKI]

## Life Period 15 — Continued Detention (2021–present, as of mid-2026)

- As of June 2026, per the most current source found in this research, she
  remains in military custody; Myanmar's military reported moving her in
  late April 2026 to a "designated residence," but her family has had no
  independent means of confirming her condition or exact location, and she
  remains physically cut off from outside contact. [ASIATIMES]
- The same source documents that, more than five years after the coup, the
  active resistance movement within Myanmar has moved substantially beyond
  her own nonviolent political framework (younger, and in many cases armed),
  even as she remains, per this account, a figure regional and global powers
  still treat as politically unavoidable — the West continuing to invoke
  her case as a point of principle, ASEAN treating diplomatic access to her
  as a live test issue, and both India and China maintaining contact with
  the junta that holds her while separately acknowledging her case.
  [ASIATIMES]

## Public-facing profile assets (structurally separate from scoring evidence)

*(Editorial-content material only — NOT evidence for scoring. Kept separate
per instructions.)*

### Key achievements
1. **Leading Burma's 1988 pro-democracy movement and co-founding the
   National League for Democracy**, moving from private citizen to the
   central figure of a national mass movement within roughly six months of
   returning to the country to nurse her mother. [WIKI, SEARCH-AGG]
2. **The 1990 election landslide and the 1991 Nobel Peace Prize**, awarded
   for "her non-violent struggle for democracy and human rights" while she
   remained under house arrest and unable to travel to accept it. [WIKI,
   HRW]
3. **Enduring roughly 15 of 21 years (1989–2010) under house arrest** rather
   than accept repeated offers of release conditioned on leaving the
   country — including declining to leave even to see her dying husband in
   1999 — becoming an internationally recognized symbol of nonviolent
   resistance to military rule. [WIKI, HRW]
4. **Leading the NLD to two further landslide election victories (2015 and
   2020)** and serving as Myanmar's de facto civilian head of government
   (State Counsellor) from 2016 to the February 2021 coup. [WIKI]

### Revealing anecdotes
1. **Walking toward the levelled rifles at Danubyu (1989).** Facing soldiers
   under direct orders to fire, she moved her own followers out of the line
   of fire while she herself continued walking straight toward the guns
   until the order was countermanded. [SEARCH-AGG]
2. **"As a mother, the greater sacrifice was giving up my sons."** Her own
   framing, after choosing not to leave Burma even as her husband lay dying
   of cancer in Oxford, comparing her own sacrifice to that of colleagues
   who had "given up more." [WIKI]
3. **Her 64th birthday in a prison cell, sharing cake with her guards
   (2009).** A small, specific, documented human detail from within an
   adversarial custodial relationship during her trial over the Yettaw
   swimming incident. [WIKI]
4. **From "Stop Genocide" protester target to sitting head-of-government
   defense counsel, in one lifetime.** Her personal appearance at the
   International Court of Justice in December 2019, arguing against
   genocide charges on behalf of the same military that had imprisoned her
   for fifteen years, while protesters outside carried her photograph
   captioned "Shame." [AJ-ICJ, multiple corroborating sources]

### Turning points
1. **The March 1988 return to nurse her mother, followed within six months
   by the Shwedagon Pagoda speech.** An unplanned family circumstance
   (her mother's stroke) placed her in Burma at the exact moment of the
   8888 Uprising, and she moved from apolitical returning expatriate to
   national opposition leader in under a year. [WIKI, POPHAM]
2. **Michael Aris's death (1999) without a final visit.** The definitive,
   irreversible cost of her earlier 1988-89 choice to remain politically
   committed inside Burma rather than preserve the option of free
   international travel. [WIKI]
3. **The August 2017 Rakhine crisis and her subsequent silence, culminating
   in her personal December 2019 ICJ appearance.** The point at which her
   international reputation shifted most sharply, from globally celebrated
   democracy icon to a leader personally and publicly defending her own
   military against genocide allegations. [JOD, AJ-ICJ]
4. **The February 2021 coup.** Ended her period as civilian head of
   government and returned her to military detention, a status that (per
   the most current source in this research) persists as of mid-2026.
   [WIKI, ASIATIMES]

### Editorial potential tags
- **"Person of the Week"** — plausible given the sheer scale/duration of
  her public story (15 years of house arrest, a Nobel Prize, a national
  election landslide, then a documented international reputational reversal
  and a second imprisonment) — though any such piece would need careful,
  even-handed framing given her contested later record.
- **"On This Day"** — strong fit for several precisely-dated episodes: 26
  August 1988 (Shwedagon Pagoda speech), 20 July 1989 (first house arrest),
  10 December 2019 (ICJ appearance), 1 February 2021 (coup/arrest).
- **"Same Traits, Different Lives"** — a natural pairing candidate against
  other long-imprisoned nonviolent-resistance figures, or against other
  leaders whose international reputation shifted sharply between an earlier
  and later career phase.
- **"Great Opposites"** — a strong candidate for pairing against figures who
  either never wavered in a single moral framing across their public life,
  or whose relationship between civilian and military power ran in the
  opposite direction (a leader who empowered civil oversight of the
  military rather than one whose own government sheltered military
  conduct from account).
- **"Unexpected Similarities"** — a plausible pairing candidate against other
  once-imprisoned dissidents-turned-heads-of-government whose governing
  record complicated their earlier reputation, without asserting any
  equivalence of moral outcome.

## Documentation gaps (for the record)

- Several of her own primary-source works (the "Freedom from Fear" essay in
  full, "Letters from Burma" in full, and the full text of both the 1988
  Shwedagon speech and the 2012 Nobel lecture) could not be fetched as
  complete primary documents in this session — the hosting pages each
  returned an HTTP 403 or timeout on direct fetch. All quotations used here
  are corroborated across at least two independent secondary sources that
  quote the same wording, but a future session with different fetch access
  could usefully verify these against a single authoritative full-text copy.
- Because she is a living person still in detention as of this research
  date (August 2026), no complete post-2021 first-person material (memoir,
  interview, or further public statement) exists to consult — nearly all
  documented material for Life Periods 14-15 is necessarily third-party
  (legal/journalistic) rather than self-reported.
- Governance-era material (Life Periods 11-13) is considerably richer and
  more specific than the earlier activist/detention-era material for
  granular day-to-day behavioral detail, reflecting that investigative/
  scholarly journalism scrutinized her conduct in government far more
  closely, in real time, than it could scrutinize her conduct while
  isolated under house arrest for most of 1989-2010.
