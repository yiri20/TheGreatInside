## Life Period 1 — Childhood, Family, Education (1907–1934)

- Born Oscar Ribeiro de Almeida Niemeyer Soares Filho, 15 December 1907, in
  Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, to an upper-middle-class family of German and
  Portuguese descent — the Niemeyer surname came from a German-Brazilian
  paternal line with roots traced to Hanover. His paternal grandfather was a
  judge on Brazil's supreme court; his father worked in the graphic-
  arts/typography trade, and young Oscar worked in his father's typography
  house before turning to architecture. He was one of six siblings and was
  raised primarily by his maternal grandparents in Laranjeiras, a quiet,
  upper-middle-class district of Rio. [WIKI; SEARCH-AGG — note: some
  secondary sources describe the father's occupation slightly differently
  (a few characterize him loosely as a professional without specifying
  the graphic-arts trade); this is flagged as a minor documentation
  variance rather than resolved definitively.]
- Niemeyer described his own youth as "bohemian and relatively unconcerned
  with his future." [WIKI]
- A documented, repeatedly-corroborated childhood detail: as a small child
  he compulsively sketched in the air with his fingers before he was given
  an actual pencil. In his own words (quoted across multiple independent
  accounts): "When I was very little, my mother said I used to draw in the
  air with my fingers. I needed a pencil. Once I could hold one, I have
  drawn every day since." Multiple biographical treatments (WR1, and
  independently the search-aggregated architects'-sketchbook material)
  connect this specific habit directly to a later-life claim that he could
  "do a project without the use of a pencil," designing mentally before
  ever touching paper. [WR1; SEARCH-AGG; VICE, corroborating] — a
  documented instance of an unusually early, self-directed, repeated
  creative practice, independently corroborated across sources rather than
  resting on a single anecdote.
- Left the Santo Antonio Maria Zaccaria priory school in 1928. At age 21, in
  1928, married Annita Baldo, the daughter of Italian immigrants from
  Padua. Their only child, Anna Maria, was born in 1929. [WIKI]
- Enrolled at the National School of Fine Arts (Escola Nacional de Belas
  Artes) in Rio de Janeiro, graduating with a degree in architecture (and,
  per some accounts, engineering) in 1934. [WIKI; WR1]

## Life Period 2 — Early Career, Mentorship Under Lúcio Costa, and Le Corbusier (1934–1943)

- After graduating in 1934, Niemeyer worked WITHOUT PAY in Lúcio Costa's
  architectural studio, alongside Gregori Warchavchik and Carlos Leão, in
  order to master draftsmanship under an established modernist. Costa —
  who championed modernist principles while respecting Brazilian colonial
  tradition — became Niemeyer's central early mentor. [WIKI] — a documented
  instance of accepting unpaid, subordinate work specifically to learn
  from someone he considered a master, rather than seeking immediately
  paid, independent work.
- 1936: Costa was appointed to design the Ministry of Education and Health
  headquarters in Rio de Janeiro and invited Le Corbusier as an
  international consultant. Niemeyer was INITIALLY EXCLUDED from the team
  but insisted on joining. During Le Corbusier's stay in Brazil, Niemeyer
  assisted with drafts and worked in close proximity to the older architect.
  His subsequent design modifications impressed Costa enough that Costa
  gradually ceded leadership of the project to Niemeyer by 1939. [WIKI] —
  a documented case of pushing his way into an opportunity he had been
  excluded from, then earning expanded authority through demonstrated
  design work rather than through seniority or being granted it upfront.
- The Ministry of Education and Health building (Palácio Gustavo Capanema)
  was completed in 1943, when Niemeyer was 36 — the first state-sponsored
  modernist skyscraper in the world, MoMA praising it (per secondary
  sourcing) as "the most beautiful government building in the western
  hemisphere." It featured azulejo tiles, adjustable brises-soleil, tropical
  gardens by landscape architect Roberto Burle Marx (beginning Niemeyer's
  long working collaboration with Burle Marx), and commissioned Brazilian
  artworks. [WIKI; WR1]
- 1939: at age 32, Niemeyer and Costa (with Paul Lester Wiener) designed the
  Brazilian pavilion for the 1939 New York World's Fair. New York Mayor
  Fiorello La Guardia awarded Niemeyer the keys to the city for the
  pavilion's avant-garde, curve-emphasizing design. Niemeyer later
  articulated the underlying aesthetic philosophy: "I am not attracted to
  straight angles or to the straight line... I am attracted to free-flowing,
  sensual curves... Curves make up the entire Universe, the curved Universe
  of Einstein." [WIKI]
- January 1943: the "Brazil Builds" exhibition opened at MoMA in New York
  (13 Jan–28 Feb 1943), bringing Niemeyer and Brazilian modernism broad
  international acclaim and reportedly prompting American architects/
  critics to ask whether the US had fallen behind Brazil in modern
  architecture. [SEARCH-AGG, MoMA's own exhibition-calendar listing
  cross-checked against multiple secondary summaries]

## Life Period 3 — Meeting Kubitschek, the Pampulha Complex (1940–1943)

- 1940, at age 33: Niemeyer met Juscelino Kubitschek, then mayor of Belo
  Horizonte. Kubitschek and state governor Benedito Valadares commissioned
  Niemeyer to design a suburban lakeside leisure complex (casino,
  restaurant/dance hall, yacht club, golf club, and a church), all completed
  by 1943 — a direct outcome of a personal relationship with a politician
  who would later become President and give Niemeyer his largest
  commission (Brasília). [WIKI]
- The Church of Saint Francis of Assisi at Pampulha structurally
  revolutionized reinforced-concrete use, unifying wall and roof into a
  single curved shell and abandoning the conventional pillar-beam-slab
  structure. It featured azulejo tiles by Cândido Portinari and murals by
  Paulo Werneck. [WIKI]
- The church's consecration was REFUSED by conservative Catholic Church
  authorities for roughly 16 years, until 1959 — specifically because of
  its unorthodox curved form and, separately, because Portinari's abstract
  Via Crucis mural/altar imagery (whose only clearly recognizable form was
  said to be a dog, possibly representing St. Francis as savior of the ill,
  poor, and sinners) did not conform to conventional Catholic iconographic
  norms. The building nonetheless later became the first officially listed
  modern building in Brazil. [WIKI; SEARCH-AGG] — a documented instance of
  a design choice provoking sustained institutional rejection (16 years) 
  rather than compromise or redesign; Niemeyer did not alter the building
  to secure faster religious approval.
- Niemeyer later credited Pampulha as the true beginning of his mature,
  curve-based style and, in his own words, "the beginning of Brasília": "I
  deliberately disregarded the right angle and rationalist architecture...
  to boldly enter the world of curves... offered by reinforced concrete."
  [WIKI]
- The 1943 "Brazil Builds" MoMA exhibition (see Period 2) brought Pampulha
  specifically to international attention.

## Life Period 4 — Mid-Career Prolific Output, Rationalist Criticism, and Self-Correction (1943–1956)

- Niemeyer became one of Brazil's most prolific working architects across
  this period: Banco Boavista headquarters (1948), Edifício Montreal
  (1951–54), Edifício Triângulo (1955), Ibirapuera Park buildings for São
  Paulo's 400th-anniversary celebration (1950, featuring innovative V-
  shaped pilotis that became widely fashionable), the Governador Juscelino
  Kubitschek housing complex (1951, roughly 1,000 apartments with W-shaped
  pilotis), and the Copan apartment tower in São Paulo (1953–1966), among
  many residential commissions including his own home, the Canoas House
  (1953), built on sloped terrain with a free-form roof on thin metal
  columns. [WIKI]
- 1947: Niemeyer returned to New York as part of the international Board of
  Design for the United Nations Headquarters, the youngest of ten board
  architects, working under Wallace Harrison's direction from early 1947
  through 1949. Niemeyer's own scheme (32) was initially the board's
  preferred design, separating the Secretariat and General Assembly into
  two towers around a large civic plaza. Le Corbusier had lobbied hard for
  his own centralized scheme (23) and had even asked Niemeyer NOT to submit
  a competing design at all, instead assisting him. Niemeyer nonetheless
  submitted his own scheme, which the board favored — but he then yielded
  to sustained pressure from the older, more established Le Corbusier and
  agreed to a merged submission (23/32) combining elements of both designs,
  rather than insisting the board's own preference (his design) proceed
  unmodified. Le Corbusier privately recorded in his own notebook a
  thumbnail sketch of his design labeled "beau" and Niemeyer's labeled
  "médiocre" — documented evidence of real private disdain beneath the
  public collaborative credit-sharing. Niemeyer's own contemporaneous
  statement about the building's purpose: "When we make a building for the
  UN, we must have in mind what is the UN? It is an organization to set
  the nations of the world in a common direction and gives to the world
  security." [UN-ARCHIVES; UN-DISPUTE] — a documented case of yielding
  ground to a senior figure's pressure even after formally winning an
  official design competition/board preference, rather than insisting on
  the position he had already secured.
- U.S. political exclusion: Niemeyer's Communist Party sympathies (see
  Period 6 for formal 1945 membership) blocked multiple planned U.S.
  academic appointments — a 1946 Yale University teaching invitation and a
  1953 Harvard Graduate School of Design deanship appointment (at age 46)
  were both blocked by U.S. visa denial specifically because of his
  political affiliation. His only significant U.S. professional presence
  remained the UN Headquarters collaboration. [WIKI; WR2]
- 1953–57: rationalist architecture critics, prominently Swiss architect
  Max Bill (writing in Manchete Magazine), attacked Niemeyer's free-form,
  curved style as merely decorative and warned it risked a "dangerous
  anti-social academicism"; Bill specifically dismissed Niemeyer's
  signature V-shaped pilotis as purely aesthetic rather than structurally
  meaningful. Niemeyer initially denied the criticism, then counter-argued
  that Bill's patronizing framing ignored Brazil's different social and
  economic realities. His mentor Lúcio Costa publicly defended Brazilian
  architecture's basis in unskilled, hand-crafted construction labor,
  contrasting it with Swiss precision-manufacturing culture. [WIKI] — a
  documented public professional dispute in which Niemeyer's first
  reaction was denial, followed by a substantive counter-argument rather
  than either silence or unconditional concession.
- Niemeyer later admitted, in the context of this same criticism episode,
  that he had "handled too many commissions, executing them in a hurry,"
  which had led to mediocre imitations of his own style by others. His
  first trip to Europe, for Berlin's 1957 Interbau exhibition (the Oscar
  Niemeyer Haus apartment building, part of postwar Hansaviertel
  reconstruction housing), profoundly affected his architectural
  philosophy. He subsequently published "Depoimento" in Módulo Magazine,
  proposing a simplified approach — discarding brises-soleil, sculptural
  pilotis, and marquees in favor of purer structural expression: "When form
  creates beauty, it has in beauty itself its justification." [WIKI] — a
  documented instance of publicly acknowledging a specific professional
  shortcoming (over-commitment leading to diluted quality) and then
  revising his own working method and stated aesthetic philosophy in
  response, rather than defending the prior approach unchanged.
- 1955: Museum of Modern Art in Caracas design — an inverted pyramid rising
  from a cliff — exemplified this revised, more minimal-massing approach.
  [WIKI]

## Life Period 5 — Brasília: Commission, Design, and Construction (1956–1960)

- September 1956: newly inaugurated President Juscelino Kubitschek visited
  Niemeyer personally at his own home, the Canoas House, and announced his
  plan directly to him: "I am going to build a new capital for this
  country and I want you to help me... Oscar, this time we are going to
  build the capital of Brazil." [WIKI] — the commission arrived through a
  pre-existing personal relationship (dating to the 1940 Pampulha
  commission) rather than a competitive, anonymous process.
- Niemeyer organized a design competition for the new capital's urban
  layout; his own friend and former mentor, Lúcio Costa, won it. Niemeyer
  then designed the city's landmark government buildings while Costa
  designed the urban plan — a formal division of labor between two
  long-time collaborators. [WIKI]
- Major Brasília buildings, essentially all completed by 1960: Palácio da
  Alvorada (presidential residence, with inverted arches over Lake
  Paranoá), the National Congress of Brazil (an asymmetrical sculptural
  complex with opposing domes), the Cathedral of Brasília (a hyperboloid
  concrete-and-glass structure, with large glass windows Niemeyer said were
  meant "to connect the people to the sky, where their Lord's paradise
  is" — notable given his own personal atheism, see Period 8), Palácio do
  Planalto, the Supreme Federal Court Palace (half-arches around an open
  glass entry), the Itamaraty Palace (combining modern glass with classical
  colonnade forms), and multiple ministry buildings. [WIKI; WR1]
- The whole city — design, construction, and inauguration — was completed
  within four years, reportedly using around 40,000 construction workers.
  [WR1]
- Political/urban-planning philosophy explicitly embedded in the design:
  all Brasília apartments were government-owned and rented to government
  employees; there were deliberately no "nobler" residential zones, so
  ministers and laborers were meant to share buildings — an architectural
  expression of socialist/egalitarian ideology. Many of these socialist
  design intentions were subsequently altered by later presidential
  administrations. [WIKI] In practice, per critical assessment (WR2), the
  city did not achieve its egalitarian housing ideal — service workers
  ended up housed in surrounding favelas while government employees
  commuted from Rio and São Paulo, and the car-centric layout produced
  emptied, under-used public plazas lacking pedestrian shading/gathering
  infrastructure. [WR2] — documented evidence that Niemeyer's/Costa's
  stated egalitarian intent for the city diverged substantially from its
  lived social outcome; not attributed to Niemeyer's personal choices
  specifically but a documented gap between his stated ideology and the
  built result he was centrally responsible for.
- Niemeyer became chief of the University of Brasília's architecture
  college. In 1963 he received honorary membership in the American
  Institute of Architects and, separately, the Lenin Peace Prize from the
  USSR. In 1987, Brasília was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site —
  the first international recognition of Niemeyer's own work granted
  during his lifetime. [WIKI]

## Life Period 6 — Communist Party Membership and Political Identity (1945 onward)

- 1945: as communist militants arrested under Getúlio Vargas's dictatorship
  were released, Niemeyer sheltered some of them at his own architectural
  office, and through this met left-wing leader Luís Carlos Prestes. He
  formally joined the Brazilian Communist Party the same year. [WIKI;
  SEARCH-AGG] — a documented instance of using his own professional space
  to materially assist people facing state repression, prior to and
  directly connected with his own formal political commitment.
- Prestes and, later, Cuban leader Fidel Castro became among his closest
  personal friends — Castro reportedly visited Niemeyer's apartment and
  studio in Brazil frequently, and is quoted as saying: "Niemeyer and I are
  the last communists on this planet." Hugo Chávez also regularly visited
  him in later years. In 2007 Niemeyer gave Castro a sculpture described
  as explicitly anti-American in content/symbolism. [SEARCH-AGG] — a
  sustained, decades-long personal friendship with a head of state built
  on shared ideological commitment, documented as a genuine reciprocal
  personal relationship rather than a purely professional or symbolic
  association.
- 1963: received the International Lenin Peace Prize from the USSR (see
  also Period 5). From 1992 to 1996, in his 80s and 90s, Niemeyer served
  as the formal president of the Brazilian Communist Party, a role
  described as providing crucial legitimacy to the party during its
  post-Soviet-collapse organizational crisis. [WIKI] — a documented,
  sustained, decades-spanning commitment to formal political-organizational
  leadership, continuing into very old age rather than remaining only a
  symbolic, younger-era affiliation.
- Contemporary and later critics noted a documented tension: his first
  major work (the Pampulha church) and Brasília's presidential/ministerial
  palaces possessed what critics called a "bourgeois" formal character
  despite his communist ideology, and — separately — while he consistently
  criticized Brazil's extreme class inequality, he also continued designing
  private homes, offices, and other commissions for wealthy clients
  throughout his career. Niemeyer's own documented response to this
  tension was not to deny the contradiction but to reframe architecture's
  actual power: "Architecture does not change anything. It's always on the
  side of the wealthy. The important thing is to believe that it can make
  life better." He separately stated architecture alone cannot fix an
  "unjust society" — that such change requires direct political action:
  "Our concern is political too — to change the world... Life is more
  important than architecture. What matters is to improve human beings."
  [SEARCH-AGG; PHILIPPOU] — a documented instance of directly
  acknowledging, rather than denying or rationalizing away, a real
  contradiction between his stated political values and his actual
  professional practice.

## Life Period 7 — Military Coup, Exile, and Overseas Work (1964–1985)

- March 1964: President João Goulart was deposed in a military coup;
  General Castelo Branco assumed power, beginning a dictatorship that
  lasted more than two decades (to 1985). Niemeyer's own architectural
  office was raided and pillaged; his existing clients reportedly began
  disappearing/withdrawing from his practice. [WIKI; WR1]
- 1965: Niemeyer and roughly 200 University of Brasília professors
  collectively resigned in protest of the military government's treatment
  of the university. A government Minister of Aeronautics reportedly
  remarked: "the place for a communist architect is Moscow." [WIKI] — a
  documented instance of collective institutional protest resignation
  rather than continuing to work quietly within an institution under a
  government he opposed.
- 1965–66: facing this direct professional persecution (office raided,
  clients lost), Niemeyer left Brazil for exile in France, opening a new
  office on the Champs-Élysées in Paris. He remained based in France for
  close to two decades (until 1985), using it as a hub for a substantially
  international client list he could not have accessed while working
  purely from Brazil. [WIKI; PARIS-UPDATE; WR2]
- Major exile-era international commissions: the headquarters of the
  French Communist Party at Place du Colonel Fabien, Paris (designed 1966,
  construction begun 1968, inaugurated 1971, exterior dome not fully
  completed until 1980) — a six-story serpentine office block on paired
  columns, with an underground auditorium beneath an exterior dome that
  Niemeyer himself described as representing a pregnant woman; the PCF
  turned to Niemeyer specifically as an exiled, committed lifelong
  communist when consolidating its Paris offices, and he donated the
  design plans. It has been a listed French historic monument since 2007
  and its architecture has become visually inseparable from the PCF's own
  public identity. [DOMUS; PARIS-UPDATE; WIKI]
- Other major exile-period work: the Bourse du Travail at Bobigny (a
  trumpet-horn-shaped auditorium interior); L'Humanité newspaper
  headquarters at Saint-Denis (completed 1978/1989, later left vacant and
  deteriorated for 14 years); the University of Science and Technology
  Houari Boumediene and other commissions in Algeria under President
  Houari Boumediene (a Foreign Office building and a sports arena, timed
  to Algeria's post-independence architectural self-assertion); the
  Mondadori publishing company headquarters near Milan, Italy; a casino in
  Funchal, Madeira, Portugal; the Maison de la Culture in Le Havre, France;
  nuclear-power-plant work and private villas on the French Côte d'Azur;
  and, remotely, two U.S. commissions designed by mail without Niemeyer
  ever visiting (a house for Anne and Joseph Strick in Santa Monica, and a
  "Beauty Pavilion" for plastic surgeon Dr. Robert Alan Franklyn on Sunset
  Boulevard, West Hollywood — later home to Mark Mothersbaugh's Mutato
  Muzika studio from 1995). Separately, the International Permanent
  Exhibition Centre in Tripoli, Lebanon (designed 1962, construction
  disrupted by the 1975 Lebanese Civil War, later declared a UNESCO World
  Heritage Site in 2023). [WIKI; PARIS-UPDATE; WR2]
- During the exile years he also collaborated with his own daughter, Anna
  Maria (an architect/designer in her own right), on modernist furniture
  design — sculptural steel-and-leather easy chairs and ottomans, later
  produced by the Tendo company back in Brazil starting in 1978. His
  furniture curves were widely described (though this specific
  interpretation was one he personally, and directly, disputed — see
  Period 9) as echoing both the female form and the hills of Rio de
  Janeiro. [WIKI; WR2]
- Personal reaction to exile, documented in his own later writing: he
  wrote a homesick poem during this period: "I want to watch the stars / I
  want to feel life / And be back in Brazil / That's where I want to
  live." [MEMOIR, via search synthesis] — first-person evidence of
  emotional distress/longing during the two-decade exile period, not
  merely an inferred hardship.
- As the dictatorship softened under President João Figueiredo's
  transitional rule toward democracy, Niemeyer returned to Brazil in 1985.
  [WIKI]

## Life Period 8 — Return to Brazil, Continued Political Role, and Late Career (1985–2012)

- Major late-career works after his 1985 return: the Memorial Juscelino
  Kubitschek (1980, designed shortly before full return); the Pantheon of
  the Fatherland and Freedom — Tancredo Neves (1985); the Latin America
  Memorial (1987) in São Paulo, which London's *The Independent* newspaper
  described critically as "an incoherent and vulgar construction" — the
  memorial features a large sculpture of a bleeding, wounded hand shaped
  like the outline of Central and South America; the Memorial dos Povos
  Indígenas (Memorial for Indigenous Peoples) and a Catedral Militar
  (Igreja de N.S. da Paz), both in Brasília; the Niterói Contemporary Art
  Museum (1996), cantilevered dramatically from a cliff face over
  Guanabara Bay; the Oscar Niemeyer Museum complex in Curitiba (2002); the
  Serpentine Gallery Summer Pavilion in Hyde Park, London (2003, when
  Niemeyer was 96); the Oscar Niemeyer International Cultural Centre in
  Avilés, Spain (2011, marking the 25th anniversary of the Prince of
  Asturias Award); the Auditorium Oscar Niemeyer in Ravello, Italy (concept
  and sketches completed by Niemeyer around 2000, opened January 2010
  under architect Domenico de Masi's on-site guidance); and the Oscar
  Niemeyer Sphere in Leipzig, Germany (2011) — reportedly his final
  design. [WIKI]
- 1988, at age 81: Niemeyer received the Pritzker Architecture Prize,
  shared that year (its tenth anniversary) with American architect Gordon
  Bunshaft — an unprecedented dual award the jury explicitly framed as
  honoring "architects from North and South America whose work has had
  worldwide influence in this century." [PRITZKER]
- 1998, at age 91: received Britain's RIBA Royal Gold Medal, its top
  architecture honor — reportedly beating out Frank Gehry and Cesar Pelli
  for the award that year. Niemeyer did not travel to London to collect
  the medal in person, due to a documented fear of flying. [RIBA] — a
  specific, concrete personal limitation (fear of flying) that materially
  affected how he engaged even with his own highest honors, rather than a
  generalized description of caution or risk-aversion.
- 1992–1996 (already noted in Period 6): served as formal president of the
  Brazilian Communist Party in his mid-80s to late-80s.
- Critical reassessment of his very late work: New York Times architecture
  critic Nicolai Ouroussoff explicitly questioned whether Niemeyer's
  advanced age had affected the quality of his later output, judging the
  Niterói Contemporary Art Museum significantly weaker than his earlier
  work and writing that "the greatest threat to Mr. Niemeyer's remarkable
  legacy may not be the developer's bulldozer or insensitive city
  planners, but Mr. Niemeyer himself." This assessment was directly
  contested by other critics — Brazilian architecture professor João Masao
  Kamita instead judged Niterói a genuine "return to form": "There was no
  way of denying it, the 'old man' had returned to form. Niterói is an
  impressive work of architecture, a perfect symbiosis between built form
  and landscape." [NYT-OUROUSSOFF] — a documented instance of serious,
  direct professional criticism of his continued output at advanced age,
  presented alongside an equally serious, directly opposing assessment,
  rather than treated as settled.
- Niemeyer was an avid, longtime cigar smoker whose consumption reportedly
  increased in his final years; his own studio remained a designated
  smoking area even as public smoking restrictions tightened elsewhere.
  [WIKI]
- On his continued work into extreme old age, aged nearly 100, he described
  his own daily routine plainly, without dramatizing it: "I just did my
  work... I tried to do the things I liked to do, lighter things, while
  working freely and always exploring technology, especially concrete,
  with all its sculptural potential. I think architecture finally is
  personal, a matter of intuition and invention." At 100 he still climbed
  the stairs each morning to a full day of work in his penthouse office in
  Rio, overlooking Sugar Loaf Mountain and Copacabana Beach, with a desk
  full of active projects. At 101, in the Vice interview, he maintained a
  fixed daily schedule: arriving around 10 a.m. for press meetings and
  discussing politics and philosophy with friends, continuing design work
  indefinitely rather than treating retirement as a live option. [SEARCH-
  AGG; VICE]

## Life Period 9 — Working Method, Aesthetic Philosophy, and a Documented Self-Contradiction

- Niemeyer explicitly rejected the Bauhaus/rationalist premise that
  architecture's value lay purely in functionalism: "Architecture has to be
  pretty. It has to amaze to be a masterpiece." [VICE]
- He described his design process, especially in later life, as
  substantially mental rather than dependent on the physical act of
  drawing: "Architecture is in my head. I am able to do a project without
  the use of a pencil." [VICE] — this is presented alongside, not
  instead of, the extensive documented record of his actual freehand
  concept sketches (croquis), which multiple independent architectural-
  history sources describe as central to how he communicated and developed
  designs throughout his career — bare-essential contour lines, occasionally
  paired with a reclined-female-figure reference sketch, meant to persuade a
  viewer the proposal was simultaneously economical and beautiful as pure
  form. [SEARCH-AGG, citing academic/museum treatments of his sketchbooks]
- Documented, specific self-contradiction on the meaning of his curves: in
  the 101-year-old Vice interview, when directly asked about persistent
  claims/rumors that his curves reference the female body, Niemeyer denied
  it outright: "The form comes from nothing" — arguing instead that his
  shapes emerged from spatial/structural calculation and only sometimes
  coincidentally resembled organic forms. [VICE] This directly CONTRADICTS
  his own earlier, repeatedly-quoted memoir language explicitly describing
  his curves as originating from "the mountains of my country," "the
  sinuousness of its rivers," "the waves of the ocean," and — per several
  independent secondary descriptions of his furniture work specifically —
  from the female form and Rio's own hills. [MEMOIR; WIKI; WR2] — this is
  flagged explicitly as a genuine, source-documented inconsistency in his
  own self-description over time (his own earlier writing vs. a later
  interview denial), not resolved here, and not attributable to
  interviewer error given the quote's direct, unambiguous framing.
- His own memoir's introduction directly acknowledges a documented dual
  temperament in his own self-assessment: "On re-reading this book, I feel
  that it uncovers two distinct personas. One looks on the bright side of
  life and sees the fun part of it that has always attracted me. The other
  has a pessimistic view of life and society in general, and is angered by
  the injustices of this world." [MEMOIR] — explicit first-person
  self-report of an internally acknowledged tension between temperamental
  optimism and social/political anger, rather than an outside
  characterization.
- Lifelong atheist, reasoning explicitly from "injustices of this world"
  and a cosmological/naturalistic framing: "It's a fantastic Universe which
  humiliates us... in the end, that's it — you are born, you die, that's
  it!" Despite this personal atheism, he designed numerous religious
  buildings across multiple faiths over his career (Catholic chapels, an
  Orthodox church, mosques), respecting the spiritual purposes of these
  commissions even while not personally sharing the underlying belief.
  [WIKI]
- He was also a signatory to agreements convening a "World Constituent
  Assembly" aimed at drafting a Constitution for a Federation of Earth — a
  documented instance of engaging with utopian/internationalist political
  projects well beyond his own professional field. [WIKI]

## Life Period 10 — Personal Life, Final Years, and Death (2004–2012)

- His wife Annita Baldo Niemeyer died in 2004, aged 93, after 76 years of
  marriage. [WIKI]
- In 2006, shortly before his 99th birthday and following a hip fracture
  from a fall, Niemeyer married his longtime secretary, Vera Lucia
  Cabreira, then 60 years old. [WIKI; SEARCH-AGG]
- His daughter, Anna Maria Niemeyer — herself an architect, designer, and
  gallery owner, and his earlier furniture-design collaborator (Period 7)
  — died of emphysema in June 2012, aged 82, roughly six months before her
  father's own death. [WIKI]
- Niemeyer died of cardiorespiratory arrest, from complications of a
  respiratory infection, on 5 December 2012, at the Hospital Samaritano in
  Rio de Janeiro, aged 104 — after having been hospitalized on and off for
  roughly a month with kidney ailments and pneumonia. [WIKI; WAPO]
- President Dilma Rousseff — whose own office sits among landmark buildings
  Niemeyer designed in Brasília — invited his family to hold his funeral
  at the Palácio do Planalto, the presidential palace itself; the family
  accepted, and the Brazilian Air Force provided a plane to carry his body
  from Rio de Janeiro to Brasília for the service. Rousseff publicly called
  him "a revolutionary, the mentor of a new architecture, beautiful,
  logical, and, as he himself defined it, inventive," adding "Brazil today
  lost one of its geniuses. It's a day for mourning." Thousands of mourners
  attended the memorial on 6 December 2012. He was buried at São João
  Batista Cemetery in Rio de Janeiro. [SEARCH-AGG] — a documented instance
  of the highest level of formal state honor (a head-of-state-hosted
  funeral at the seat of government he himself designed) granted at death,
  consistent with his sustained, decades-long personal and professional
  proximity to Brazilian political leadership going back to Kubitschek.
- Career-spanning summary figure: over a 78-year working career, Niemeyer
  designed approximately 600 projects. The BBC called him one of "the most
  innovative and daring architects of the last 60 years." *The Washington
  Post* called him "widely regarded as the foremost Latin American
  architect of the last century." NYT critic Nicolai Ouroussoff's own
  obituary described his forms as "curvaceous, lyrical, hedonistic," a
  "counterpoint to reductive notions of modernist architecture as blandly
  functional," writing that his work stood as "a symbolic reminder that
  the body and the mind, the sensual and the rational, are not necessarily
  in opposition." [WIKI; WAPO; NYT-OUROUSSOFF]

### Key achievements
1. **Pampulha architectural complex and the Church of São Francisco de
   Assis (1940–43).** Working from a personal relationship with Juscelino
   Kubitschek, Niemeyer used reinforced concrete to unify wall and roof
   into single curved shells, discarding the pillar-beam-slab convention —
   a design so unorthodox the Catholic Church itself refused to consecrate
   the resulting church for 16 years, yet the complex is now UNESCO World
   Heritage-listed and Niemeyer himself called it "the beginning of
   Brasília." [WIKI]
2. **Co-design of the United Nations Headquarters, New York (1947–49).**
   As the youngest member of an international ten-architect design board,
   his own scheme was the board's initially preferred design before he
   yielded ground to Le Corbusier's pressure for a merged submission — an
   early, globally visible credit for a Latin American architect on the
   most prominent international commission of the era. [UN-ARCHIVES;
   UN-DISPUTE]
3. **Designing the government/civic core of Brasília, Brazil's purpose-
   built new capital (1956–60).** Working again alongside Lúcio Costa
   (urban plan) at Kubitschek's direct personal invitation, Niemeyer
   designed the Presidential Palace, National Congress, Cathedral, Supreme
   Court, and ministry buildings that remain the city's defining landmarks
   — completed in four years and inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site
   in 1987. [WIKI]
4. **Two decades of continued, internationally distributed architectural
   output despite political exile (1965–1985), and continued major
   commissions into his 10th decade.** Rather than his career ending with
   his 1965 exile from Brazil, Niemeyer produced major buildings across
   France, Algeria, Italy, Portugal, and Lebanon, then returned to Brazil
   in 1985 and continued designing significant public buildings (Niterói
   Contemporary Art Museum, the Serpentine Gallery Pavilion at 96, his
   final design in Leipzig at 104) until his death. [WIKI; PARIS-UPDATE]

### Revealing anecdotes
1. **"I needed a pencil."** As a small child, before he could hold a pencil,
   Niemeyer reportedly drew constantly in the air with his fingers — a
   detail his own mother recounted and that multiple independent
   architectural-history treatments connect directly to his later,
   much-repeated claim that he could design "in his head," without ever
   touching paper. [WR1; VICE; SEARCH-AGG]
2. **Building his rival's disdain into his own greatest early credit.**
   Niemeyer's own UN Headquarters scheme was the design board's preferred
   choice, but Le Corbusier — who had asked Niemeyer not to even submit a
   competing design — pressured him into a merged submission; Le Corbusier
   privately labeled his own sketch "beau" and Niemeyer's "médiocre" in his
   own notebook, a private judgment at odds with the board's own
   professional assessment. [UN-DISPUTE]
3. **A fear of flying that kept him from his own highest honors.** At 91,
   Niemeyer won Britain's RIBA Royal Gold Medal but did not travel to
   London to accept it in person, reportedly because of a genuine fear of
   flying — a small, human limitation set against a career built on
   international commissions he mostly directed from Brazil or, later,
   Paris rather than by personally traveling to each site. [RIBA]
4. **Denying his own most repeated interpretive claim, at 101.** Asked
   directly whether his signature curves referenced the female body — a
   claim repeated in his own furniture-design coverage and consistent with
   his own memoir's language about curves drawn from nature — Niemeyer
   flatly denied it: "The form comes from nothing." [VICE; MEMOIR]

### Turning points
1. **Insisting his way onto the Ministry of Education team after being
   excluded (1936).** Rather than accepting his initial exclusion from
   Costa's high-profile Le Corbusier-consulted commission, Niemeyer pushed
   to join, then earned expanded design authority through the quality of
   his own work rather than through seniority — arguably the template for
   his entire subsequent career pattern of using demonstrated design skill
   to win larger and larger roles. [WIKI]
2. **The 1964 military coup and 1965 exile.** Rather than the Brazilian
   dictatorship ending his career, the raid on his office and loss of
   Brazilian clients redirected two decades of his working life toward an
   international, France-based practice that produced some of his most
   politically significant work (the French Communist Party headquarters)
   — a career-defining redirection driven by political persecution rather
   than professional choice. [WIKI; PARIS-UPDATE]
3. **The Max Bill criticism and the 1957 Berlin/Interbau trip (early-to-
   mid 1950s).** Public professional criticism that his free-form style was
   merely decorative, followed by his own admission of having overextended
   himself across too many rushed commissions, directly preceded a
   deliberate, self-initiated simplification of his design philosophy
   (published as "Depoimento") — a documented instance of criticism and
   self-recognized overreach producing genuine methodological revision
   rather than defensive continuation. [WIKI]
