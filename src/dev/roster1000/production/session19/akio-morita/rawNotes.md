by life period. NOT organized around any trait taxonomy — this is raw

## Life Period 1 — Family, Childhood, and Education (1921–1944)

- Born 26 January 1921 in Nagoya, into a family that had operated sake,
  miso, and soy-sauce production in Kosugaya village (now Tokoname City,
  Chita Peninsula, Aichi Prefecture) for roughly 300 years. Sources
  variously describe him as the fourteenth or fifteenth generational heir
  to this business — the exact generation count is not perfectly
  consistent across secondary accounts, treated as approximate here.
  [WIKI; ENCYC; EBSCO]
- Eldest of four siblings. Father Kyuzaemon (also rendered Kyusaemon)
  groomed him from childhood specifically to inherit the family brewing
  enterprise — a direct, stated expectation, not an assumed one. [WIKI;
  ENCYC]
- His mother, Shuko, had a documented passion for Western classical music
  and owned one of the first RCA Victrola phonographs in Japan —
  independently identified by ENCYC as the specific formative influence
  that pulled young Morita's interest toward sound reproduction and
  electronics rather than the family trade. [ENCYC]
- As a child/teenager he became consumed by building his own ham (amateur)
  radio equipment and by electrical devices generally (vacuum tubes, sound
  reproduction). This was serious enough to measurably hurt his schoolwork:
  multiple sources independently describe him as having "almost flunked
  out of school" from single-minded focus on electronics, before
  disciplining himself to concentrate on his studies for roughly a year —
  after which he entered the prestigious Eighth Higher School as a physics
  major. [ENCYC; EBSCO cross-check] — a documented instance of an early,
  self-recognized period of unbalanced focus followed by a deliberate,
  self-imposed correction, rather than either continued neglect or
  external intervention forcing the change.
- Studied applied physics at Osaka Imperial University (1940–1944) under
  named professor Tsunesaburo Asada. [EBSCO] Graduated 1944 with a physics
  degree. [WIKI, cross-checked]
- Chose physics over the family business specifically — a documented
  divergence from his father's explicit expectation, resolved in favor of
  his own technical interest well before Sony existed as an idea. [WIKI;
  ENCYC]

## Life Period 2 — Wartime Naval Research and Meeting Ibuka (1944–1945)

- Rather than wait to be conscripted as the war intensified, Morita
  volunteered for the Imperial Japanese Navy. [ENCYC] Commissioned as a
  sub-lieutenant/technician-lieutenant in the engineering corps upon
  graduating in 1944. [WIKI; EBSCO]
- Assigned to a Navy study group / the Aviation Technology Center,
  supervising and assisting research into thermal/infrared-guided weapons
  (the Ke-Go infrared-guided bomb program) and night-vision gunsights for
  the Wartime Research Committee. [WIKI; ENCYC — both converge on this
  same wartime research assignment]
- It was in this specific wartime research context that Morita met Masaru
  Ibuka, an electronics engineer roughly 13 years his senior who was
  already running an electronics-parts factory employing about 1,500
  people. [WIKI; MEMOIR via search synthesis, corroborated] — the
  founding relationship of his entire subsequent career began as a
  professional wartime-research colleague relationship, not a personal or
  family connection.

## Life Period 3 — Founding Totsuko and Early Product Failures (1945–1952)

- September 1945 (immediately after Japan's surrender): Ibuka, on his own
  initiative, opened a radio-repair shop in the bombed-out ruins of the
  Shirokiya Department Store in Nihonbashi, Tokyo. [WIKI; ENCYC]
- Morita learned of this venture by reading a newspaper article about it
  and joined Ibuka — a documented instance of actively seeking out and
  re-establishing a wartime professional connection once peacetime
  circumstances made a joint venture conceivable, rather than the two men
  simply staying in touch. [WIKI, corroborated via MEMOIR search synthesis]
- 7 May 1946: the two men founded Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo Kabushiki Kaisha
  ("Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering Corporation," Totsuko for short)
  with approximately 20 employees. Startup capital is given inconsistently
  across sources as either ¥190,000 or a rough $500 equivalent — likely
  reflecting different exchange-rate-era conversions of the same original
  figure, treated as approximate. Capital was a loan from Morita's own
  father — i.e., his family's sake-brewing wealth directly financed the
  venture he chose instead of inheriting that business. [WIKI; ENCYC;
  EBSCO; MEMOIR — all converge on the founding date/structure]
- Ibuka personally drafted the company's founding prospectus (7 May 1946),
  which explicitly stated the purpose of incorporation was "to create a
  free-spirited and enjoyable ideal factory that contributes to Japanese
  culture through excellent technology" — an idealistic, not primarily
  profit-oriented, founding charter that both men are documented as having
  operated under in the company's earliest years. [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated
  across multiple independent secondary sources]
- Early products included amplifiers and communication equipment supplied
  to Japanese government agencies, before any consumer product existed.
  [ENCYC]
- Early, documented product FAILURE: the company attempted to build an
  electric rice cooker — a wooden bucket with electrodes at the bottom
  designed to cut power once the water had fully steamed away. The design
  was clever in concept but did not work reliably in practice: it
  routinely under-cooked or over-cooked the rice, and the product did not
  succeed commercially. [MEMOIR via search synthesis, cross-checked against
  multiple independent secondary retellings] — a documented early failure
  in the company's very first product-development attempts, notable
  because it long predates any of the company's later successes and shows
  the founders working, at first, largely by trial and error outside their
  actual engineering competence (audio/electronics, not home appliances).
- First real consumer product: a reel-to-reel magnetic tape recorder,
  developed 1949–1950 (the company developed its own recording tape in
  1949) and released as Japan's first tape recorder in 1950. [WIKI; EBSCO]
  The initial retail unit was expensive (sources cite a rough $500
  equivalent) and did not sell well to ordinary consumers. [ENCYC]
- Rather than abandon the tape recorder after weak general-consumer
  sales, Morita personally identified and pursued an institutional market:
  he personally demonstrated the recorder to Japanese schools, arguing for
  its educational-dictation use, and this direct-demonstration approach
  succeeded to the point that nearly one-third of Japan's elementary
  schools purchased units. [EBSCO] — a documented instance of redirecting
  a product toward an institutional buyer himself, hands-on, after the
  originally intended consumer market did not respond, rather than
  discontinuing the product.
- In the late 1940s, a young musician/baritone named Norio Ohga (then
  still a student) wrote Totsuko a sharply critical letter about the poor
  sound quality ("wobbly," effectively describing the machine as
  inadequate/"junk" for a professional singer's needs), using the specific
  analogy that "a ballet dancer needs a mirror to perfect her style… a
  singer needs the same, an aural mirror." Rather than being offended,
  Morita/the company recognized Ohga's ear for sound fidelity and brought
  him in as a part-time consultant (from around 1953), then a full
  employee (1959) — Ohga went on to become Sony's later CEO/chairman
  (from 1989/1994) and is separately credited as an architect of the CD
  format. [NATHAN via search synthesis; SEARCH-AGG cross-check] — a
  documented instance of responding to sharp, unsolicited outside
  criticism of the company's own product by recruiting the critic rather
  than dismissing him.

## Life Period 4 — The Transistor Gamble and Renaming to Sony (1952–1958)

- 1952: Ibuka personally traveled to the United States to explore
  acquiring rights to the newly invented transistor from Western Electric
  Company (WEC), the patent holder. [MEMOIR via search synthesis]
- Western Electric's asking licensing fee (cited as $25,000, in one
  account equal to roughly a full year's company profits at the time) was
  a large, genuinely risky commitment for a still-small company. At the
  time, transistors were widely viewed in the electronics industry as
  suited mainly to hearing aids, not viable for general consumer
  electronics — meaning the company was betting its own capital and
  credibility on a technology application most contemporaries doubted.
  [MEMOIR/EBSCO/ENCYC — all three converge on this] — a documented
  instance of committing a large, hard-to-recover sum of capital to a
  technology whose most obvious contemporary application (hearing aids)
  was far narrower than what the company actually intended to build.
- After the licensing agreement was reached (1953), Japan's Ministry of
  International Trade and Industry (MITI) — the government body
  controlling foreign-currency remittance — reportedly "dallied six
  months" before finally releasing the foreign exchange needed to actually
  pay the licensing fee, i.e. Japanese officialdom itself was actively
  skeptical of or at least unhurried about the transaction. Morita later
  drew a general lesson from this specific delay, stating that
  "government often impedes innovative change and developments by
  excessive intervention." [ENCYC — a specific, dated bureaucratic-
  obstruction episode with Morita's own generalized reflection attached]
- Sony (still Totsuko at the time) introduced Japan's first transistor
  radio in 1955, followed by the world's first pocket-sized transistor
  radio in 1957. [EBSCO; WIKI]
- Documented marketing pragmatism/deception around the "pocket-sized"
  radio: because the actual radio was slightly too large for a standard
  men's shirt pocket, Morita instructed sales staff to wear specially
  tailored shirts with deliberately oversized breast pockets during sales
  demonstrations, so the radio would visually appear to be genuinely
  pocket-sized to prospective buyers and retailers. [WIKI; MEMOIR via
  search synthesis, cross-checked] — a small but concrete, documented
  instance of engineering the buyer's immediate physical/visual impression
  of a product rather than only its underlying engineering.
- 1958: the company formally renamed itself "Sony," a name Morita and
  Ibuka deliberately invented rather than adopted — combining the Latin
  "sonus" (sound) with the American colloquial nickname "sonny," chosen
  specifically because it was pronounceable and memorable in essentially
  any language/alphabet, including for buyers who could not read or say
  "Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo." The two founders reportedly searched dictionaries
  looking for exactly this kind of universally pronounceable name. [WIKI;
  ENCYC; SEARCH-AGG] — a deliberate, foundational branding risk: choosing
  to abandon a functioning, if clunky, Japanese company name in favor of a
  wholly invented, non-Japanese-sounding word specifically to support a
  global rather than domestic identity, years before the company had any
  major international sales to justify it.

## Life Period 5 — Global Expansion and the Move to New York (1960–1972)

- 1960: Morita founded Sony Corporation of America to establish and run
  U.S. operations directly, rather than selling through existing American
  distributors or under an American brand name. He explicitly rejected
  outside offers to market Sony's products under U.S. company names,
  insisting on building Sony's own brand identity abroad from the start.
  [EBSCO; ENCYC]
- Sony America's first American showroom opened on Fifth Avenue in
  Manhattan; the U.S. operation ran unprofitably for roughly its first
  decade before becoming a major contributor (by the 1960s, over one
  million Sony micro-televisions had been sold in the U.S.). [EBSCO] — a
  documented instance of sustaining a loss-making foreign operation for
  roughly ten years on the belief that direct market presence would
  eventually pay off, rather than retreating to a licensing/distributor
  model after early unprofitability.
- 1961: Sony became the first Japanese company to list its stock in the
  United States, via American Depositary Receipts (ADRs) on the New York
  Stock Exchange. [WIKI; EBSCO; TIME]
- 1962 (per EBSCO) / 1963 (per other search-aggregated sources — the
  exact year is not perfectly consistent across accounts): Morita
  relocated to the United States together with his wife and three
  children specifically to manage American operations directly and to
  personally learn and internalize American business culture and consumer
  thinking, rather than directing U.S. strategy remotely from Tokyo. His
  own stated rationale, per a widely quoted passage: "I believe it is a
  mistake to open a factory overseas without first having a sales and
  marketing system established and knowing the market very well… you must
  first learn the market, learn how to sell to it, and build up your
  corporate confidence before you commit yourself." [EBSCO; SEARCH-AGG] —
  a documented, explicit statement of his own reasoning for a major,
  disruptive personal/family decision (uprooting his family to a foreign
  country) tied directly to a stated business-strategy principle.
- Having observed that American professionals routinely moved between
  employers mid-career — a practice essentially unheard of in Japan's
  norm of single-company lifetime employment at the time — Morita, on
  returning to Japan, began encouraging and hiring experienced mid-career
  professionals away from competitor firms into Sony, a practice that
  reportedly influenced wider Japanese industry norms afterward. [WIKI] —
  a documented instance of observing a foreign professional norm during
  his U.S. immersion and then deliberately importing it into Japanese
  corporate practice against the prevailing convention, notwithstanding
  that Sony's OWN internal culture (per other sources, see Period 6) is
  separately described as emphasizing long-term loyalty and a
  "family-like" employment relationship — a genuine, documented tension
  between the two practices rather than a fully consistent employment
  philosophy.
- 1966: joint venture between Sony and CBS Records established (Morita as
  president of the venture), producing content library alongside Sony's
  hardware — a first, smaller-scale precedent for the much larger, later
  Columbia Pictures acquisition (see Period 8). [EBSCO]
- 1971: Sony's Rancho Bernardo plant near San Diego, California began
  producing Trinitron television sets for the U.S. market — by 1981
  employing roughly 1,800 people and producing about 750,000 sets
  annually. Management at this plant blended Japanese and American
  practices in what was informally called "the San Diego way": a
  deliberately non-hierarchical structure, monthly manager-worker
  meetings, responsive attention to worker welfare issues (e.g. quickly
  replacing unsatisfactory employee-housing refrigerators), leading to
  rejected unionization votes and documented worker loyalty. Sony
  explicitly did NOT force American workers to adopt Japanese management
  norms wholesale, instead adapting practice to the local workforce.
  [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated across multiple independent secondary
  accounts] — a documented case of adapting an underlying management
  philosophy to a different cultural/labor context rather than exporting
  the Japanese model unchanged.
- 1971: Morita became president of Sony Corporation, succeeding Ibuka
  (who had led as president 1950–1971); Ibuka became chairman. Ibuka
  formally retired from the chairman role in 1976, at which point Morita
  became chairman and CEO. [WIKI; EBSCO; ENCYC — succession dates
  cross-checked and consistent across all three]

## Life Period 6 — Product Philosophy and the Walkman Decision (1975–1982)

- 1975: Sony released the Betamax, the first home videocassette-recorder
  format, one year ahead of JVC's rival VHS format (1976). [WIKI; EBSCO]
- Morita made a deliberate strategic decision that Betamax would be sold
  ONLY under the Sony brand name — refusing to license the format to
  other manufacturers or retailers who wanted their own branding, in
  contrast to JVC's rivals, who licensed VHS broadly to many companies
  (including, notably, adult-film distributors, an early and lucrative
  driver of home-video-rental demand that Sony's exclusivity policy
  effectively locked itself out of). [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated across
  multiple independent secondary sources] — a specific, high-stakes,
  self-attributed strategic misjudgment: Morita himself later
  acknowledged that this licensing restriction slowed Betamax's ecosystem
  growth and created the opening VHS exploited, even though other
  commentators point to additional contributing factors (Betamax's
  initial one-hour recording limit versus VHS's longer runtime, and JVC's
  earlier/deeper relationships with American film studios and video-rental
  outlets) as at least equally important.
- Betamax ultimately lost the format war to VHS despite reaching the
  market first and being regarded as higher video quality — a documented,
  large-scale commercial defeat that Sony (and Morita personally, as the
  decision-maker behind the exclusive-licensing policy) had to publicly
  absorb over the following decade. [SEARCH-AGG]
- February 1979: Morita assembled a team and set an aggressive four-month
  deadline to develop and launch a compact, playback-only portable stereo
  cassette player in time for the summer travel/vacation season.
  [COMMONCOG]
- The product (which became the Walkman, initially the TPS-L2) faced
  documented, near-unanimous internal opposition BEFORE launch: Sony's own
  market-research department warned that customers would reject a tape
  player with no recording function; the sales division pointed out that
  no prior Sony tape deck had ever sold more than fifteen thousand units,
  while the required volumes for the new product's economics were roughly
  double that; marketing objected that the name "Walkman" itself was
  broken/embarrassing English; and engineers said the four-month
  production timeline Morita demanded was not achievable. [COMMONCOG;
  SEARCH-AGG cross-check — both converge]
- Facing this internal opposition, Morita personally told Sony's board
  that he would resign if the product failed. [COMMONCOG; SEARCH-AGG] —
  first-person-attributable evidence of personally staking his own
  position on a single, contested product decision, a high-stakes bet
  made against the near-unanimous judgment of his own specialist
  departments (market research, sales, marketing, and engineering all
  raised distinct objections).
- Morita's own later, explicit reasoning (per his memoir) for overriding
  the market research: he did not believe any amount of market research
  could have predicted the Walkman's eventual success, framing his own
  role as anticipating a need consumers themselves could not yet
  articulate ("The public does not know what is possible, but we do").
  [COMMONCOG; MEMOIR via search synthesis — self-reported reasoning,
  flagged as such] — an explicit, stated rejection of consumer-research-led
  product development in favor of his own judgment/intuition, a
  consequential and generalizable stance, not limited to this one product.
- During development, in response to a specific documented internal
  concern (reportedly including his own wife feeling excluded/rude during
  early testing when only one person could listen via headphones at a
  time), Morita insisted on adding a second headphone jack and a "hot
  line" button that let two people share a single unit and talk to each
  other over lowered music volume — a design compromise aimed at social
  acceptability that consumers, once the product launched, ultimately
  ignored almost entirely (Sony later quietly dropped both features).
  [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated across multiple independent accounts] —
  documents a specific instance where his own social/etiquette intuition
  about how the product WOULD be used turned out to be wrong, even though
  his broader bet that the category itself would succeed was vindicated.
- Launch: 2 June 1979, via an unconventional live demonstration in a
  Tokyo park rather than a traditional press conference — Sony staff
  distributed units to journalists and demonstrated the product being used
  on skateboards and tandem bicycles. Initial sales were lukewarm (about
  3,000 units in the first month), a real, documented near-term signal
  that the bet might be failing before targeted advertising drove rapid
  acceleration and the initial 30,000-unit batch sold out by August 1979.
  [COMMONCOG] — i.e., Morita's confidence was not immediately vindicated by
  the market; there was a real period where the product appeared to be
  underperforming before the eventual, much larger success (186 million
  units sold by the 20th anniversary; roughly 200 million cassette-based
  units over the product line's full ~30-year run) became evident.
- 1982: Sony (having jointly developed the format with Philips, first
  publicly shown at CES 1981) launched the CD player, and separately
  introduced the 3.5-inch floppy-disk physical format that became a de
  facto industry standard. [WIKI; EBSCO; CES] — Norio Ohga (the same
  Ohga recruited via his critical letter in Period 3) is separately
  credited elsewhere as a key technical architect of the CD standard's
  specific parameters (e.g. disc playing time), illustrating a
  documented, recurring pattern of Morita's earlier personnel decisions
  paying off in later, unrelated technical achievements.

## Life Period 7 — Books, Political Voice, and Trade-Friction Controversy (1966–1991)

- 1966: Morita authored *Gakureki Muyō Ron* ("Never Mind School Records"
  / "School-Record Irrelevance Theory"), arguing that Japan's rigid
  emphasis on academic credentials/pedigree was not the true determinant
  of business success or capability — a public position at odds with
  prevailing Japanese social convention at the time, published while he
  was already a prominent, credentialed (physics-degree, elite-university)
  businessman himself. [WIKI, corroborated via search synthesis]
- Late 1980s: Morita became an outspoken public voice in the escalating
  US–Japan trade friction of the period, including the semiconductor
  trade disputes. He publicly argued that manufacturing itself (not
  finance or services) is the essential foundation of a national economy,
  telling Wharton School graduates in a 1990 address: "I am not a believer
  in the idea of a post-industrial society… the very essence of business
  is manufacturing" — a direct, public criticism of the American economy's
  drift away from manufacturing. [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated via UPI
  Archives coverage of the same Wharton address]
- 1989–91: Morita co-authored *The Japan That Can Say No: Why Japan Will
  Be First Among Equals* with nationalist politician Shintarō Ishihara.
  Morita's own contributed chapters argued Japan should abandon its
  postwar economic/diplomatic deference toward the United States and
  should be willing to use its economic leverage — e.g. control over
  advanced semiconductor supply — more assertively in bilateral relations,
  including regarding US military basing in Japan. [SEARCH-AGG,
  corroborated across multiple independent academic/press sources] — a
  clear, public, documented instance of a private businessman taking an
  assertive geopolitical/nationalist policy position well outside his
  own corporate mandate.
- The book, originally aimed at a domestic Japanese readership, generated
  unauthorized English translations that circulated in the United States
  and caused a significant public controversy there — American press and
  officials reacted to Morita's chapters as unexpectedly combative for a
  businessman whose company depended heavily on American consumers and
  goodwill. [SEARCH-AGG] Facing this backlash, Morita had his own chapters
  REMOVED from the eventual 1991 authorized English-language edition
  (translated by Frank Baldwin, Simon & Schuster) — only Ishihara's
  material appeared in the official English version — and Morita
  subsequently distanced himself from the book's American reception,
  later stating he had not intended the controversial framing his chapters
  were read as making. [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated across multiple
  independent sources including academic citation of the unauthorized
  vs. authorized translation difference] — a documented instance of taking
  a public, assertive political position, then reversing/retracting that
  public stance once it produced serious reputational/commercial backlash,
  rather than maintaining it under pressure.
- By the early-to-mid 1990s, per a further, separately corroborated
  statement, Morita's own public assessment was that "Japan bashing" in
  the U.S. was subsiding and that Americans were increasingly focused on
  "fixing the American problem" rather than blaming Japan — a documented
  softening/moderation of his own earlier confrontational public position
  as circumstances changed. [SEARCH-AGG]

## Life Period 8 — Institute/Company Leadership Style, Columbia Pictures, and the Ibuka Partnership (1971–1998)

- Throughout his tenure as president/chairman, Morita is documented (via
  his own memoir and independently via Nathan) as having built Sony's
  internal culture around "teamwork," employee "challenging work in a
  family-like environment," and treating "workers not as tools but as
  fellow human beings" — explicit, repeated rhetoric distinguishing his
  stated management philosophy from a purely transactional employment
  relationship. [ENCYC; MEMOIR via search synthesis]
- He specifically criticized American corporate management's tendency to
  focus on quarterly profits/dividends at the expense of long-term
  capital investment, positioning Sony's practice of investing roughly
  six to ten percent of annual sales into R&D — deliberately outside any
  government subsidy or the large industrial-conglomerate (*keiretsu*)
  structures common in Japan — as the correct alternative model. [ENCYC]
- His own memoir describes an internal company suggestion system under
  which employees were expected/encouraged to submit multiple ideas a
  year ("eight suggestions yearly" in one specific passage), and describes
  cross-training scientists into sales roles for a period specifically so
  technical staff would understand customer-facing realities directly
  rather than only through intermediaries. [MEMOIR via search synthesis —
  self-reported management practice]
- His own memoir also candidly states that he learned "an enemy of
  innovation can be your own sales organization" — a documented,
  first-person acknowledgment that internal organizational resistance
  (not only external market conditions) was a recurring obstacle he had
  to personally overcome to bring new products to market (directly
  echoing the later, better-documented Walkman episode in Period 6).
  [MEMOIR via search synthesis]
- His four-decade partnership with Ibuka is independently characterized
  (Nathan; TIME; the Springer academic case chapter) as a clean,
  complementary division of labor: Ibuka as the hands-on engineering
  "dreamer" who would personally take gadgets apart on his office floor,
  Morita as the internationally-facing business "pragmatist"/diplomat who
  built relationships across three continents. Despite the very different
  temperaments, the two men's bond is described by Ibuka's own son (at
  Ibuka's 1998 funeral, per Morita's wife's own quoted words) as being
  "closer than lovers" — i.e., an unusually close personal, not merely
  professional, relationship sustained across more than 40 years. [TIME;
  NATHAN via search synthesis]
- Nathan's independent account specifically argues that several major
  Sony decisions were driven as much by personal loyalty/affection within
  this inner circle as by hard strategic calculation — most pointedly, the
  1989 ~$4.7 billion Columbia Pictures Entertainment acquisition (following
  the smaller 1987 CBS Records purchase) is characterized as having been
  pursued, in significant part, because senior Sony executives wanted to
  fulfill Morita's own long-standing personal desire to own a movie
  studio, even though the acquisition went on to cost Sony billions of
  dollars and is widely regarded as a costly strategic misstep. [NATHAN
  via search synthesis] — an independent (non-memoir) source's specific,
  critical assessment that a major, costly corporate decision was
  substantially motivated by loyalty to Morita as a person rather than a
  purely rational business case — a genuinely different, more critical
  lens than Morita's own memoir or the encyclopedia entries provide.

## Life Period 9 — Health Decline and Death (1993–1999)

- 30 November 1993 (per one specific dated account; the exact date is
  not perfectly consistent across all secondary sources, some giving only
  "late 1993"): while playing tennis, Morita suffered a cerebral
  hemorrhage (stroke), requiring brain surgery and ending his active,
  hands-on management role at Sony. The stroke left him significantly and
  permanently weakened, partially paralyzed, and largely wheelchair-
  dependent for the rest of his life. [WIKI; ENCYC; SEARCH-AGG]
- Despite this, per the encyclopedia sources, Morita remained mentally
  sharp and continued to be consulted on major company matters — most
  notably, he is documented as having approved Norio Ohga's selection of
  Nobuyuki Idei as the next successor CEO, valuing Idei's marketing
  background and European business experience specifically. [ENCYC] — a
  documented instance of continuing to exercise real institutional
  influence and judgment from a severely diminished physical state, rather
  than being entirely sidelined once physically incapacitated.
- His post-stroke rehabilitation reportedly included alternating practice
  speaking in Japanese and English on different days, as part of his
  recovery therapy. [ENCYC]
- 25 November 1994: Morita formally stepped down as Sony chairman and
  CEO, becoming honorary chairman; Norio Ohga (recruited decades earlier
  via his own critical letter — see Period 3) succeeded him as chairman.
  [WIKI; ENCYC]
- Morita spent much of his remaining time recuperating in Hawaii with his
  wife Yoshiko Kamei (whom he had married 13 May 1950; they had three
  children — Hideo, Masao, and Naoko). [ENCYC; WIKI cross-check]
- Died 3 October 1999, in a Tokyo hospital, of pneumonia, at age 78 —
  having been hospitalized since around August 1999; his stroke-weakened
  condition in his final years had already prevented any return to active
  leadership. [WIKI; SEARCH-AGG cross-check]
- Received extensive contemporaneous honors reflecting sustained
  recognition during his lifetime, not only posthumous reputation:
  Golden Plate Award, American Academy of Achievement (1972); Albert
  Medal, Royal Society of Arts — the first Japanese recipient (1982);
  Legion of Honour, France (1984); First Class Order of the Sacred
  Treasure, Emperor of Japan (1991); election, American Philosophical
  Society (1992); fellowship, American Academy of Arts and Sciences, and
  honorary British knighthood (KBE) (1993); named the only Asian person
  on *Time* magazine's list of the 20 most influential 20th-century
  business leaders (1998); posthumous Grand Cordon of the Order of the

## Public-facing profile assets (structurally separate from scoring evidence)

*(Editorial-content material only — NOT evidence for scoring. Kept separate
per instructions.)*

### Key achievements
1. **Co-founding Sony from the ruins of postwar Tokyo (1946) and building
   it into a global brand.** Starting from a bombed-out department store
   with roughly 20 employees and a family loan, Morita and Ibuka built a
   company that, by the time of Morita's death, was a global electronics
   and entertainment conglomerate with tens of billions of dollars in
   annual sales. [WIKI; EBSCO]
2. **The transistor-radio gamble (1953–57).** Committing a large, hard-to-
   recover licensing fee to Western Electric for a technology most of the
   industry considered viable only for hearing aids, then following
   through to produce the world's first pocket-sized transistor radio —
   a foundational bet that made Sony a genuine electronics innovator
   rather than a postwar imitator. [MEMOIR; EBSCO; ENCYC]
3. **The Walkman (1979), launched over the near-unanimous objection of
   Sony's own market research, sales, marketing, and engineering
   departments** — Morita personally staked his position on the product,
   telling the board he would resign if it failed; it went on to sell
   roughly 200 million cassette-based units over three decades and
   redefined personal music listening worldwide. [COMMONCOG]
4. **Building "Made in Japan" into a mark of quality rather than cheap
   imitation** — through direct international distribution, an invented,
   deliberately non-Japanese brand name ("Sony"), and sustained personal
   presence in the U.S. market from 1960 onward, Morita is widely credited
   with helping change global perception of Japanese manufacturing more
   broadly, not only of his own company's products. [EBSCO; CES]

### Revealing anecdotes
1. **The oversized shirt pocket.** When Sony's new "pocket-sized"
   transistor radio (1957) turned out to be slightly too large for a
   standard men's shirt pocket, Morita had sales staff wear specially
   tailored shirts with enlarged pockets during demonstrations so the
   radio would visually read as pocket-sized to buyers — a small,
   concrete instance of managing the buyer's immediate physical impression
   of a product. [WIKI; MEMOIR via search synthesis]
2. **Recruiting his own harshest critic.** A young musician, Norio Ohga,
   wrote Totsuko a scathing letter about its tape recorder's poor sound
   quality, comparing the need for accurate audio reproduction to a
   dancer needing a mirror. Rather than dismiss the criticism, Morita's
   company brought Ohga in as a consultant — he eventually became Sony's
   own CEO and chairman decades later. [NATHAN via search synthesis]
3. **"I will resign if it fails."** Facing near-unanimous internal
   opposition to the Walkman from his own specialist departments, Morita
   told Sony's board he would resign personally if the product failed —
   a concrete, first-person stake placed on a single contested product
   decision. [COMMONCOG]
4. **Renaming the whole company to a made-up word.** Rather than adopt an
   existing word in any language, Morita and Ibuka searched dictionaries
   for a name that would be pronounceable everywhere and settled on the
   invented "Sony" (Latin "sonus" + American "sonny") — years before the
   company had major international sales to justify a global-facing
   identity. [WIKI; ENCYC]

### Turning points
1. **Choosing physics over the family sake business (1930s–1944).** An
   early, foundational divergence from an explicit paternal/family
   expectation, resolved in favor of his own technical interests well
   before any version of Sony existed. [WIKI; ENCYC]
2. **Meeting Ibuka during wartime infrared-weapons research (1944–45).**
   The single relationship on which his entire subsequent career was
   built began as a professional wartime research colleague connection,
   re-activated by Morita's own initiative (reading a newspaper article
   about Ibuka's postwar radio-repair venture) rather than by any prior
   personal tie. [WIKI; ENCYC]
3. **The 1993 stroke and 1994 retirement.** A sudden, severe health event
   that permanently ended his active leadership role, yet — per the
   documented record — did not fully end his institutional influence, as
   he continued to be consulted on succession planning even from a
   wheelchair-bound, partially paralyzed state. [ENCYC]

## Documentation gaps (for the record)

- Morita's own memoir, *Made in Japan* (1986), and John Nathan's
  independent *Sony: The Private Life* (1999) were both engaged only
  through excerpts/reviews and multiple independent, mutually
  corroborating secondary descriptions rather than full primary-text
  reading (a print monograph in both cases, and the memoir specifically
  is out of scope for a single WebFetch). The Springer Nature academic
  case-study chapter was located but sits behind an institutional login
  wall and was engaged the same way. Every specific factual/reasoning
  claim drawn primarily from these three works is corroborated here
  against at least one other independently-fetched source (Encyclopedia.
  com, EBSCO, Commoncog, CES.tech, or Time) except where explicitly
  flagged inline as resting on the memoir's own self-report alone (e.g.
  his stated reasoning for moving to New York, and his stated reasoning
  for overriding market research on the Walkman).
- The exact generational count for the family sake-brewing inheritance
  (14th vs. 15th generation) and the exact founding-capital figure
  (¥190,000 vs. a ~$500 equivalent) are both given inconsistently across
  otherwise-reliable secondary sources — treated as approximate rather
  than exact for that reason, consistent with how the Louis Pasteur
  session (session 18) handled a similar minor numeric inconsistency (the
  ENS "bean revolt" resignation count).
- Interior/emotional material is comparatively thin for Morita compared
  to, e.g., Pasteur's notebook-based revisionist scholarship — Morita's
  own memoir is candid about business reasoning and philosophy but is a
  curated corporate document, not a private diary, and no equivalent of
  Geison's notebook-based revisionist scholarship on Pasteur exists in
  the public record for Morita. Nathan's book is the closest thing to an
  independent, warts-and-all account, and even that is noted by its own
  reviewers as still broadly admiring rather than adversarial in tone.
- Detail on Morita's specific personal reaction/emotional state around the
  Betamax defeat, the Columbia Pictures acquisition's poor outcome, or his
  own stroke is thin in the sources actually available here — these are
  documented as events and (for Betamax) as a policy Morita himself later
  acknowledged as a mistake, but first-person emotional reflection on any
  of the three is not present in the sources consulted.
