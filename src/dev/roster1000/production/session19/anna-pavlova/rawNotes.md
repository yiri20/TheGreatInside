## Life Period 1 — Childhood and Discovery of Ballet (1881–1891)

- Born 12 February 1881 (some sources give 31 January, Old Style calendar
  discrepancy) in Saint Petersburg, in the Preobrazhensky Regiment
  hospital. Her mother, Lyubov Feodorovna, was a laundress/washerwoman
  from peasant stock, working for banker Lazar Polyakov. [WIKI; ENC2]
- Her paternity is genuinely disputed in the historical record: some
  accounts describe her father Matvey Pavlovich Pavlov as a former
  soldier who married her mother shortly before or after her birth;
  Vladimir Polyakov (the employing banker's son) later claimed she was
  illegitimate. Her stepfather/father figure Matvey Pavlov died when she
  was about two years old, after which she and her mother lived in real
  poverty. [WIKI; ENC2] — the family's economic circumstances at birth
  and in early childhood were genuinely difficult, not merely modest.
- Born prematurely and sickly; sent for a period to live with her
  grandmother in the village of Ligovo, apparently for her health. Frail
  health persisted as a recurring feature throughout her life, not just
  in infancy. [WIKI; EBSCO]
- At age eight or nine (sources vary — ENC1/EBSCO say nine, others eight),
  her mother took her to see Marius Petipa's production of *The Sleeping
  Beauty* at the Imperial Maryinsky Theatre. This single performance
  crystallized her ambition to become a dancer — described across
  multiple sources as the formative, decisive moment of her artistic
  vocation. [WIKI; ENC1; EBSCO]
- She auditioned for the Imperial Ballet School at around this age and
  was REJECTED — judged too young and too physically frail/weak/sickly
  in appearance. [ENC1; WIKI] — a documented early rejection specifically
  on grounds of physical unsuitability, not lack of effort or interest.
- She was accepted a year later, in 1891, at age ten. [WIKI; ENC1; EBSCO]

## Life Period 2 — Imperial Ballet School Training (1891–1899)

- The Imperial Ballet School ran a rigorous seven-year training program.
  Contemporary and later accounts describe the institution's atmosphere
  as monastic/"iron discipline" — Pavlova herself later compared it to a
  monastery. Casual contact with boys was forbidden; some described it as
  a "silken prison." [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated across multiple
  independent secondary accounts]
- Documented daily schedule: a bell at 8am, dressing under the "stern
  eye" of a governess, prayers, breakfast (tea, bread, butter), then
  morning devoted to ballet lessons, afternoons to academic subjects
  (with theatre-relevant emphasis: French, dramatic art, music).
  [SEARCH-AGG]
- Her body did not match the era's preferred compact/athletic ballet
  physique: severely arched feet, thin ankles, elongated limbs. Fellow
  students mocked her, giving her the nicknames "The broom" and "*la
  petite sauvage*" (the little savage/wild girl). [ENC1; HH]
- Rather than being deterred by this peer mockery or the physical
  mismatch with prevailing aesthetic norms, she pursued intensive
  additional study under a sequence of premier instructors: Christian
  Johansson, Pavel Gerdt, Nikolai Legat, and — most influentially —
  Enrico Cecchetti (founder of the Cecchetti method, still taught
  today). [ENC1; HH; EBSCO]
- Documented direct quote attributed to Cecchetti about her: "I can teach
  everything connected with dancing, but Pavlova has that which can only
  be taught by God." [HH]
- Her own documented philosophy, stated directly (multiple independent
  sources give this as a direct quotation): "No one can arrive from being
  talented alone. God gives talent, work transforms talent into genius."
  [ENC1; HH]
- A specific documented instance of her teacher pushing back against her
  own ambition: when she attempted to imitate Pierina Legnani's famous
  fouetté turns (a marker of high, "acrobatic" technical virtuosity), her
  teacher Pavel Gerdt reacted with anger, telling her: "leave acrobatics
  to others. It is positively more than I can bear to see the pressure
  such steps put on your delicate muscles and the severe arch of your
  foot. I beg you to never again try to imitate those who are physically
  stronger than you. You must realize that your daintiness and fragility
  are your greatest assets." [ENC1, WIKI cross-check] — direct
  documented evidence that at least one authority figure believed she was
  pushing her own physical limits past what was safe/wise, and that her
  response to this specific caution was accepted (she did not become
  known as a fouetté specialist).
- By 1898 she entered Ekaterina Vazem's elite *classe de perfection*,
  graduating in 1899 at age eighteen. Official debut in Pavel Gerdt's
  *Les Dryades prétendues* drew specific critical praise (from critic
  Nikolai Bezobrazov). [ENC1]
- Company debut at the Maryinsky Theatre, 1 September 1899, after
  completing the full seven-year program. [EBSCO]
- Documented daily practice regimen during and after school years: five
  to six hours of rehearsal per day at the Maryinsky, supplemented by
  private lessons in St. Petersburg and, later, in Italy. [ENC2; EBSCO]
  She continued to seek out Cecchetti for refresher study specifically to
  perfect her technique even after leaving formal training, and returned
  to him after extended tours. [EBSCO] — a documented, longitudinal
  pattern of ongoing, self-initiated additional training rather than
  treating graduation as the end of skill development.

## Life Period 3 — Rise Through the Imperial Ballet / Mariinsky (1899–1906)

- Graduated directly into the rank of *coryphée*, bypassing the ordinary
  *corps de ballet* entry rank — an unusual, accelerated placement.
  [ENC1; SEARCH-AGG]
- Marius Petipa, the Imperial Ballet's dominant choreographer/ballet
  master, became her most significant mentor and, per multiple sources,
  his personal favorite pupil — he personally taught her the title role
  in *Paquita*, *Giselle*, Queen Nisia in *Le Roi Candaule*, and Princess
  Aspicia in *The Pharaoh's Daughter*, and revised numerous *grands pas*
  specifically to showcase her particular qualities. [ENC1; SEARCH-AGG]
- Her dancing style was, from early on, distinctly UNorthodox relative to
  Petipa's own strict academic classicism: she often performed with bent
  knees, imperfect turnout, and unconventional port de bras — qualities
  that (per later critical assessment) evoked Romantic-era ballet's
  ethereal/expressive quality rather than the crisp technical precision
  the Imperial school otherwise prized. [ENC1]
- A specific documented instance of technical over-exuberance leading to
  a public mishap: during a performance as the River Thames in *The
  Pharaoh's Daughter*, her vigorous double pique turns caused her to
  fall into the prompter's box. [ENC1]
- Documented instance of practical, unauthorized creative initiative: her
  weak ankles created difficulty with a specific variation (the fairy
  Candide in *The Sleeping Beauty*) as originally choreographed, so she
  revised the choreography herself to suit her own physical limitations —
  a modification that reportedly startled Petipa (i.e., it was not
  something she asked permission for in advance). [ENC1]
- Achieved rank of *danseuse* in 1902, *première danseuse* in 1905, and
  full *prima ballerina* in 1906 following a triumphant performance in
  *Giselle*. Fans who followed her specifically called themselves the
  "*Pavlovatzi*." [ENC1; WIKI]

## Life Period 4 — Michel Fokine and *The Dying Swan* (1905–1909)

- Collaborated with choreographer Michel Fokine, who in 1905 (one source,
  EBSCO, gives 1907 for the premiere — treated here as a minor date
  discrepancy across secondary sources, not fully resolved) created for
  her the four-minute solo *The Dying Swan*, set to Camille Saint-Saëns's
  "Le Cygne" from *The Carnival of the Animals*. [ENC1; EBSCO]
- This became inseparably identified with her for the rest of her
  career — she performed it many hundreds/thousands of times across her
  touring life, and it became the piece invoked at her death (see Life
  Period 8). [ENC1]
- Separately, she personally self-choreographed *The Dragonfly*,
  performing it in gossamer gowns with attached wings — direct evidence
  of her own creative/choreographic initiative distinct from being purely
  an interpreter of others' work. [ENC1]
- She kept a pet swan named Jack in later years at her English home and
  was documented as having studied his real movements specifically to
  inform her performance of the Dying Swan role — connecting a real,
  personal, longitudinal engagement with an animal to the authenticity of
  a signature stage role. [WIKI; ENC1]

## Life Period 5 — Ballets Russes, Diaghilev, and the Break to Independence (1909–1913)

- Briefly performed with Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes during its
  celebrated 1909 Paris debut season (Saison Russe), appearing in works
  including Fokine's *Les Sylphides*, with set designs by Alexandre
  Benois. [VAM; ENC1]
- She was originally cast as the lead in Fokine's *The Firebird* but
  rejected the role specifically because she could not reconcile herself
  with Igor Stravinsky's avant-garde musical score — the part instead
  went to Tamara Karsavina. [ENC1] — documented evidence of firm artistic
  taste/conservatism overriding what would otherwise have been a
  career-defining role in one of the most historically significant new
  ballets of the era.
- More broadly, she was documented as preferring the more traditional,
  melodic "*musique dansante*" of nineteenth-century composers (Cesare
  Pugni, Ludwig Minkus) and was skeptical of the Ballets Russes' more
  experimental artistic departures from ballet tradition. [ENC1]
- She refused to return for a second Ballets Russes season. [ENC2]
- Documented rivalry with fellow ballerina Tamara Karsavina: per one
  secondary film-documentary source (*A Portrait of Giselle*), during one
  performance Karsavina experienced a wardrobe malfunction (exposed
  breast), which Pavlova allegedly exploited publicly to humiliate her —
  this is single-sourced in the research conducted here (via ENC1's
  synthesis of that documentary) and is flagged as weaker,
  less-corroborated evidence than the rest of this record.
- Separately documented, better-corroborated rivalry/competitiveness
  episode: in 1912, Pavlova slapped her dance partner Mikhail Mordkin
  during a curtain call, reportedly because she believed he was receiving
  more audience applause than she was. This occurred despite (or
  alongside) real professional success together — the pair received ten
  curtain calls at a 1910 London Palace Theatre performance. [ENC1; HH]
- 1911: her manager and eventual husband Victor Dandré (an aristocrat and
  council member who had followed her career since her school days, whom
  she had met around 1900–1904) faced embezzlement/fraud charges in
  Russia. Pavlova personally requested that her own earnings be used to
  post his 35,000-ruble bail. [ENC2] This financial crisis, combined with
  her stated desire for greater artistic freedom, is directly identified
  by this source as a precipitating factor in her decision to leave the
  security of the Maryinsky and pursue independent touring — a riskier
  but (per the same source) ultimately much more lucrative path. [ENC2]
- Dandré's ongoing legal/financial troubles in Russia also, per a separate
  source, prevented the couple from returning to Russia at all —
  effectively confining their base of operations to London going
  forward. [WIKI, Victor Dandré article]
- 1912: Pavlova and Dandré purchased Ivy House on Hampstead Hill (North
  End Road), Golders Green, London — her home for the rest of her life,
  featuring an ornamental lake with swans. [ENC2; WIKI]
- 1913: formally resigned from the Imperial Ballet and founded her own
  independent touring company — described directly by a source
  synthesizing multiple historical accounts as "a very enterprising and
  daring act." [ENC1] She would tour independently for the remaining
  ~18 years of her life/career, "everywhere in the world that travel was
  possible." [ENC1]

## Life Period 6 — Building and Leading Her Own Touring Company (1913–1930)

- Assembled her company primarily from young English and other European
  dancers (rather than continuing with an all-Russian troupe), training
  roughly half a dozen English girls herself each year at Ivy House, then
  giving them Russianized stage names and promoting the strongest to
  supporting roles. [ENC2; ENC1]
- Under Dandré's management, the troupe's scale grew substantially over
  time — from around six to around sixty performers. [WIKI, Dandré
  article]
- Documented management/temperament pattern, described across multiple
  independent secondary sources: "tantrums, refusals to perform, and
  sudden dismissals alternated with lavish parties and unexpected
  generosity" — i.e., a genuinely volatile, high-intensity leadership
  style rather than a uniformly calm one. [ENC1, SEARCH-AGG corroboration]
- Repeatedly described as maintaining an exceptionally demanding personal
  work ethic that she extended to her company's operating rhythm: "For
  the first decade, Pavlova herself appeared twice daily, assisted by one
  other ballerina." Her "stamina and work ethic were legendary." [ENC1]
- The company toured, per various tallies across sources, somewhere
  between roughly 300,000 and 400,000+ miles over the touring years
  (figures vary slightly by source and by which years are counted — not
  fully reconciled here, treated as approximate), performing in venues
  well outside the normal theatrical circuit: "gymnasiums, music halls,
  warehouses, even bullfighting arenas." [ENC1; EBSCO; ENC2]
- Specific 1929 Australian tour detail (the most granular touring record
  found in this research): the company (~42 dancers, larger than her
  1926 tour) sailed to Australia; on arrival their ship was advised not
  to disembark passengers at Darwin, so the tour instead began in
  Townsville, followed by Mackay, Rockhampton and Bundaberg — regional
  North Queensland towns with no prior history of ballet performance —
  before continuing to Brisbane, Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide and Perth.
  Over four months the company gave 120 performances; Pavlova, then
  aged 48, danced in every single one of them. [AUSD]
- She personally trained new company dancers in classical technique
  while also expanding her own repertoire well beyond conventional
  Western classical ballet: she learned and performed "ethnic"-inspired
  dances (Mexican, Japanese, East Indian) picked up from local teachers
  during her travels, staged a pointe-shoe version of the Mexican Hat
  Dance (*Jarabe Tapatío*) that became popular enough that Mexico
  declared it a national dance in 1924, and produced a 50-minute
  abridged *Sleeping Beauty* in New York in 1916. [ENC1]
- 1922 India visit: her husband Dandré is documented as having initially
  dismissed traditional Indian *nautch* dance as "crude and clumsy," but
  Pavlova herself was struck by the Ajanta cave frescoes and by a Hindu
  wedding ceremony she attended, and commissioned a ballet, "Ajanta
  Frescoes," inspired by the frescoes. Critics judged this specific
  production "amateurish" — a documented public artistic failure/
  overreach into unfamiliar cultural material, not simply a triumph.
  [SAHA]
- Despite that specific failure, the same India visit led to a
  substantial and durable positive legacy: introduced by musician
  Commalata Bannerjee to Uday Shankar (then a young Indian art student in
  London who also danced), she had Shankar choreograph and dance in "A
  Hindu Wedding" and later "Krishna and Radha," partnering with her
  directly on stage — an "accidental" discovery (per the source's own
  framing) that is credited with launching Shankar's career and, by
  extension, a major strand of the modern Indian classical/contemporary
  dance revival. [SAHA]
- She also personally mentored aspiring dancer Leila Sokhey (later famous
  as "Madame Menaka"), who went on to found her own dance school in
  Bombay, and met Rukmini Devi Arundale aboard a ship bound for Australia
  in 1929, for whom she personally arranged ballet instruction. [SAHA]
- She attempted to correspond with Rabindranath Tagore about creating a
  ballet based on his poetry; this specific project never materialized.
  [SAHA] — a documented instance of an ambitious creative outreach that
  did not pan out, alongside the ones that did.
- 1928–29: presented "Oriental Impressions" (including the Indian-themed
  ballets) during her India tour, "received extremely well." [SAHA]
- Solved a genuine physical/technical problem in her own footwear: added
  hard leather to the soles of her pointe shoes and flattened the box, an
  innovation that (per the historical record) eventually influenced and
  helped standardize modern pointe-shoe construction — though she was
  reportedly vain enough about how this looked that she had these
  modifications removed/retouched from published photographs of her feet.
  [WIKI cross-check via ENC1's synthesis]
- 1928: engaged conductor Efrem Kurtz to accompany her performances.
  During her final years, soloist Cleo Nordi (formerly of the Paris
  Opéra) became her dedicated on-tour assistant, including the 1929
  Australian tour; Nordi went on to teach in London for decades
  afterward, extending Pavlova's technical legacy well beyond her own
  lifetime. [WIKI]
- Ambiguity about her marital status with Dandré was, per one source,
  something she deliberately cultivated rather than clarified: she "gave
  different accounts of the exact nature of their relationship," publicly
  stated at one point that "a true female artist must be consumed in her
  art" (implying she had refused marriage), yet told reporters in 1924
  that she and Dandré had in fact been married for years, supposedly
  since 1914 — no marriage certificate has ever been located. [ENC2] —
  the record here shows a documented, sustained pattern of controlled
  ambiguity about a specific personal fact across many years, not a
  single inconsistent statement.

## Life Period 7 — Personality, Interests, and Public Persona

- Multiple sources converge on an unusually narrow personal focus: she
  reportedly "showed no interest in anything but her next performance"
  and was, per contemporaries quoted in one source, "considered by many
  people to be unintelligent," apparently lacking conversational depth
  outside the subject of dance. [NWE] — flagged as resting on a single
  source among those consulted here, though consistent in spirit with the
  broader "total absorption in the work" pattern other sources describe
  (e.g., EBSCO's "there was nothing in her life that did not contribute
  in some way to her perfection of her dancing").
- By contrast, she was widely praised by critics and collaborators
  specifically for interpretive/emotional depth in performance — Sir
  Frederick Ashton (later Britain's most celebrated 20th-century
  choreographer, who credited seeing her dance in Lima, Peru at age
  thirteen as his own formative moment) specifically noted her
  "exceptionally expressive hands and feet" and the "sensitivity and
  unique 'plasticity' of her movement." [ENC2; ENC1] Her particular
  artistic gift was described as an ability to genuinely "appear actually
  to become" whatever she portrayed — flower, dragonfly, swan, or a human
  emotional state — rather than simply performing steps accurately.
  [ENC2]
- She maintained an extensive menagerie of animal companions throughout
  her adult life — Siamese cats, dogs, birds, and her pet swans
  (especially "Jack") at Ivy House — documented in both her husband's
  own writing and in period photography/portraiture that she
  commissioned. [WIKI; ENC1]
- After WWI, she undertook sustained, personally funded charitable work:
  established a home at Saint-Cloud (near Paris) housing fifteen Russian
  orphan girls displaced by the war, funded through her own performance
  earnings plus donations (including from the Camp Fire Girls of
  America). [WIKI, Dandré-sourced material]
- Believed strongly in film as a means of preserving and teaching dance
  technique for posterity, rather than viewing it as beneath live
  performance — she personally conducted recording experiments in
  multiple countries (filming in Java, slow-motion recording in
  Australia, sound recording in Hampstead) specifically to document her
  own repertoire, working with figures including Lee de Forest (sound
  pioneer) and Douglas Fairbanks. [VAM]

## Life Period 8 — Final Illness and Death (December 1930 – January 1931)

- Continued performing almost without interruption to the very end of
  her life: her final performance was on 13 December 1930. [ENC1]
- While touring/traveling (accounts vary slightly: rehearsing/catching
  cold in Paris, then traveling on to the Netherlands for scheduled
  performances), she fell seriously ill. One source specifically states
  she insisted on traveling to the Netherlands to keep her scheduled
  performance commitments AGAINST her doctor's explicit objections about
  her mounting fever. [ENC2] She summoned her personal physician
  (Zalewski/Zalevsky) from Paris. [WIKI]
- Diagnosed with pleurisy/pneumonia (sources use both terms) at the
  Hotel Des Indes in The Hague. Doctors informed her that survival would
  require an operation, and that even if she survived the operation she
  would likely never be able to dance again. [SEARCH-AGG, corroborated
  across multiple independent sources]
- She refused the surgery. Her own documented words, quoted across
  multiple independent sources: "If I can't dance, I'd rather be dead."
  [HH; NWE; SEARCH-AGG] — an explicit, first-person statement directly
  connecting her own survival decision to her identity as a performer,
  made at the actual moment of a real, irreversible, life-or-death
  choice, not a hypothetical or retrospective claim.
- One source additionally describes a medical intervention that did
  occur before her death — doctors "pierced her back to drain fluid from
  her lungs" — suggesting some treatment was attempted even without the
  full operation she refused; the exact sequence/scope of what she
  refused vs. what she accepted is not perfectly reconciled across
  sources. [ENC2]
- Died at half past midnight on Friday, 23 January 1931 (one source gives
  22 January — a minor date discrepancy across secondary accounts, not
  resolved here), twenty days (or about a week, per another source)
  short of her fiftieth birthday, with her husband Victor Dandré, her
  maid Marguerite Létienne, and her physician present. [WIKI; ENC1]
- Per Dandré's own account (published in his 1932 memoir and repeated
  across every secondary source consulted here), her last words were:
  "Get my 'Swan' costume ready." One source additionally attributes a
  further, final line: "Play that last measure softly." [WIKI; ENC1]
- Her body was dressed in her favorite beige lace dress with a sprig of
  lilac placed in the coffin; a Russian Orthodox priest performed prayers.
  [WIKI]
- Following ballet tradition, her company's scheduled performance
  proceeded as planned that evening, with a single spotlight circling an
  empty stage while the orchestra played the Saint-Saëns music from *The
  Dying Swan*. [WIKI; ENC1; ENC2]
- Cremated at Golders Green Crematorium, near her London home; her ashes
  remain there. Later, politically motivated attempts (including one led
  by a Moscow official in the early 2000s) to relocate her remains to
  Novodevichy Cemetery in Russia, framed as fulfilling her "dying wish,"
  were found to have no supporting evidence — Dandré's own will only
  permitted such a transfer conditionally (if Russian authorities would
  treat her remains reverently) and contained no actual request for one;
  neither her family nor, ultimately, the Russian government sanctioned
  the move, and the most recent attempt (2001) was abandoned. [WIKI]

## Public-facing profile assets (structurally separate from scoring evidence)

*(Editorial-content material only — NOT evidence for scoring. Kept separate

### Key achievements
1. **Becoming Prima Ballerina of the Imperial Ballet (1906)**, rising from
   a physically "unconventional" (by contemporary standards), initially
   rejected ten-year-old student to the highest rank in the world's most
   prestigious ballet institution within fifteen years, via relentless
   additional training under multiple master teachers. [ENC1, HH]
2. **Creating and defining the enduring identity of "The Dying Swan"
   (1905)**, a four-minute solo that became arguably the single most
   famous piece in the history of solo ballet performance, performed by
   her many hundreds of times over a quarter-century. [ENC1]
3. **Founding and personally leading an independent global touring
   company (1913–1931)** that brought live ballet, for the first time,
   to audiences across six continents — including remote regional towns
   with no prior exposure to Western theatrical dance — logging several
   hundred thousand miles of travel and thousands of performances at a
   time when international travel itself was arduous and slow. [ENC1;
   AUSD; EBSCO]
4. **Discovering/launching Uday Shankar's career and mentoring multiple
   other Indian dancers (Menaka, Rukmini Devi Arundale) during her
   1922–1929 India tours** — an "accidental" cultural encounter that had
   a durable, independently-verifiable effect on the modern revival of
   Indian classical/contemporary dance, well beyond her own art form.
   [SAHA]

### Revealing anecdotes
1. **"The broom" who became prima ballerina.** Mocked as a child for a
   body shape ("the broom," "la petite sauvage") that didn't fit the era's
   ballet aesthetic, she turned that same unconventional physicality
   (extreme arch, long limbs) into the signature quality critics later
   celebrated as uniquely her own. [ENC1; HH]
2. **The teacher who told her to stop trying to be strong.** Her own
   teacher, Pavel Gerdt, became angry when she attempted virtuosic
   fouetté turns, telling her plainly that her "daintiness and fragility"
   — not raw technical power — were her real assets, and to stop trying
   to imitate physically stronger dancers. [ENC1]
3. **Slapping her own partner over applause.** In 1912, during a curtain
   call after a performance with Mikhail Mordkin, she slapped him — a
   documented, specific instance of open, physical competitive jealousy
   toward a collaborator, immediately following genuine shared professional
   triumph (ten curtain calls in London two years earlier). [ENC1; HH]
4. **Paying her manager's bail with her own career earnings.** When
   Victor Dandré faced embezzlement charges in 1911, she used her own
   money to post his 35,000-ruble bail — a decision entangled with, and
   arguably catalytic for, her subsequent break toward financially
   riskier independent touring. [ENC2]
5. **"Get my swan costume ready."** Dying of pleurisy in a Hague hotel
   room, having just refused an operation that might have saved her life
   because it would have ended her ability to dance, her documented last
   words asked for the costume of the role that had defined her entire
   career. [WIKI; ENC1; HH]

### Turning points
1. **Seeing *The Sleeping Beauty* at age eight or nine (c. 1889–90).** A
   single theatrical performance, attended with her mother, is credited
   across every source consulted as the origin of her entire vocation —
   from a sickly child in a poor St. Petersburg household to a
   world-famous performer.
2. **The 1911 Dandré embezzlement crisis.** A personal/financial
   emergency involving her manager (and later husband) directly
   coincided with, and per one source directly precipitated, her
   decision to leave the security and prestige of the Imperial
   Ballet/Maryinsky for the much riskier, self-financed path of
   independent international touring.
3. **The 1922 India tour.** A single trip intended primarily as a
   performance tour led to both a documented public artistic failure
   ("Ajanta Frescoes," judged amateurish) and, in the same trip, an
   accidental discovery (Uday Shankar) with a far larger and more
   durable positive cultural legacy than the ballet itself — success and
   failure emerging from the very same episode.

### Editorial potential tags
- **Person of the Week**: strong candidate — visually iconic (swan
  costume, Ivy House swans), a clean single-sentence "why she matters"
  (globalized ballet as a touring solo entrepreneur), and a poignant,
  widely-known death anecdote.
- **On This Day**: strong candidate for 12 February (birth) and 23
  January (death) — both dates carry ready-made, emotionally resonant
  narrative hooks.
- **Same Traits, Different Lives**: plausible pairing candidate against
  other independent-touring/company-founder figures, or against other
  figures whose signature single work overshadowed a much broader body
  of work.
- **Unexpected Similarities**: plausible pairing against a figure known
  for total absorption in one craft at the expense of broader
  intellectual range, or against a figure whose closest personal
  relationship was also their professional manager.
- **Great Opposites**: plausible pairing against a figure celebrated for
  institution-building longevity/succession planning rather than
  personal-brand-dependent leadership, since her company's identity was
  inseparable from her own body and could not really outlive her in the
  same form.

## Documentation gaps (for the record)

- Neither of the two book-length primary biographies most central to her
  life — Victor Dandré's 1932 memoir and Keith Money's 1982 *Anna
  Pavlova: Her Life and Art* — was accessible as full text for this
  research (monograph-only; the Internet Archive copy of Money's book is
  borrow-restricted with no readable preview). Both are engaged only
  indirectly, via other sources that name, cite, or quote them
  (particularly Encyclopedia.com source #2's bibliography, and the
  widely-repeated "get my swan costume ready" quotation attributed
  specifically to Dandré's account). This mirrors this project's existing
  precedent for paywalled/monograph-only scholarly sources.
- The single most personality-revealing claim in this record — that she
  "showed no interest in anything but her next performance" and was
  "considered by many people to be unintelligent" (New World
  Encyclopedia) — rests on one source among those consulted here. It is
  broadly consistent with the "total absorption in dance" pattern several
  other sources independently describe in milder language, but the
  specific, more pointed "considered unintelligent" characterization is
  not independently corroborated and should be treated cautiously at the
  scoring stage.
- The Karsavina wardrobe-malfunction/humiliation anecdote is sourced to a
  single documentary film as reported via one secondary aggregator, and
  is comparatively weak evidence (flagged inline above in Life Period 5).
- Exact dates show minor, unreconciled discrepancies across independent
  secondary sources in several places (birth date given as either 12
  February or 31 January; *The Dying Swan*'s premiere given as either
  1905 or 1907; death date given as either 22 or 23 January 1931; the
  age at which she first saw *The Sleeping Beauty* given as either eight
  or nine) — treated as approximate rather than exact for these
  specific points, consistent with this project's existing practice for
  historical figures with imperfect date reconciliation across sources
  (cf. the Louis Pasteur research's treatment of the ENS "bean revolt"
  headcount).
- Documentation of her precise day-to-day management behavior toward her
  own company (specific dismissal incidents, specific rehearsal-discipline
  confrontations) is thinner than documentation of her own personal
  training discipline — the record supports a general, well-corroborated
  characterization ("tantrums, refusals to perform, and sudden
  dismissals") but few single, fully narrated individual incidents beyond
  the Mordkin slap.
