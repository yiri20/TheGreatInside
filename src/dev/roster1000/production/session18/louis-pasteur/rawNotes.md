## Life Period 1 — Childhood, Family, Education (1822–1847)

- Born 27 December 1822 in Dole, Jura, France, to a Catholic family; father
  Jean-Joseph Pasteur was a tanner. [WIKI]
- Father Jean-Joseph was a sergeant-major in Napoleon's army, decorated with
  the Legion of Honour for his Napoleonic War service. After his gruelling
  daily work tanning hides, he would sit with young Louis and discuss
  patriotism, honour, justice, and duty to society — a documented influence
  historians connect to Pasteur's later intense French patriotism. [WIKI,
  corroborated across multiple secondary biographical treatments located via
  search]
- Family relocated to Marnoz (1826) then Arbois (1827). [WIKI]
- As a child/adolescent he was described as an average student, more drawn
  to fishing and sketching than academics. He had genuine artistic talent:
  at about age 15 he produced pastel portraits of his parents and
  neighbours, considered good enough that they were later preserved in the
  Pasteur Institute's own museum. His father viewed this artistic ability as
  a "dalliance" and hoped instead that Louis would land a teaching post at a
  local college. [WIKI, corroborated via search synthesis of multiple
  secondary biographical accounts]
- 1838: attempted boarding school in Paris but returned home early, homesick.
  [WIKI]
- 1839: enrolled at Collège Royal in Besançon; 1840 earned Bachelor of
  Letters degree. [WIKI]
- 1842: completed Bachelor of Science in Mathematics (Dijon) — but with a
  specifically mediocre grade in chemistry, the very subject that would
  define his career. [WIKI; independently corroborated via search of
  general biographical sources]
- Initially FAILED the entrance exam for the elite École Normale Supérieure
  despite preparing with extra lessons in math/physics/chemistry. Rather
  than abandon the goal, he moved to Paris (1843), enrolled at Lycée
  Saint-Louis, won the school's top-physics-student prize, and retook the
  ENS entrance exam — passing with a high rank. Entered ENS in 1844, aged
  21. [WIKI, corroborated via search synthesis] — a documented instance of
  recovering from an early academic failure through additional, deliberately
  targeted preparation rather than either giving up or blaming the exam.
- 1847: submitted two doctoral theses, one in chemistry and one in physics.
  [WIKI]

## Life Period 2 — Early Career: Crystallography, Marriage, First Posts (1847–1857)

- 1847–1854: working as a laboratory assistant/junior academic, Pasteur
  investigated why crystals of tartaric acid salts rotated polarized light
  differently depending on their source (natural vs. synthetic), even though
  chemically identical. [SHI]
- The method was described as painstaking, manual, and visually meticulous:
  he examined the mysterious tartrate crystals under a microscope and
  discovered they existed as two distinct, mirror-image crystal forms; he
  then physically separated the two crystal types by hand (using tweezers,
  per the broader historical record) based on tiny asymmetric facets visible
  only under magnification. [SHI; general chirality-history search summary]
- 1848: published the finding that one set of separated crystals rotated
  polarized light exactly as natural (+)-tartaric acid did, while the other
  set rotated it in the opposite direction — establishing that the same
  molecule could exist in two mirror-image ("chiral"/asymmetric) structural
  forms. This became the founding discovery of stereochemistry/molecular
  asymmetry. [SHI; general search synthesis on chirality history] — an
  instance of a discovery built from unusually careful, repetitive, manual
  observation rather than a single flash of insight.
- 1846: professor of physics, Collège de Tournon (Ardèche). [WIKI]
- 1848: professor of chemistry, University of Strasbourg. [WIKI]
- 29 May 1849: married Marie Laurent, daughter of the University of
  Strasbourg's rector, having proposed via a formal letter to her father
  dated 10 February 1849. [WIKI; corroborated via search synthesis on Marie
  Pasteur]
- Marie Laurent became a genuine long-term working collaborator, not merely
  a spouse: she served as his secretary/amanuensis, transcribing his
  laboratory notes "with meticulous care," managing his correspondence, and
  directly assisting in experiments — including the silkworm-disease
  investigations of the 1860s (see Period 4). Their marriage lasted 46
  years, until his death. [Search synthesis, Marie Pasteur sourcing]
- 1852: named chair of chemistry (Strasbourg). 1854: named dean of the new
  faculty of sciences, University of Lille. [WIKI]

## Life Period 3 — Fermentation Studies & the Spontaneous Generation Controversy (1854–1864)

- At Lille (from 1854), Pasteur began investigating industrial fermentation
  problems (relevant to the region's beet-alcohol/beer industry) and, by
  1857–58, published findings that living yeast microorganisms caused
  fermentation and that different microorganisms produced different
  fermentation products (e.g., lactic acid) — directly contradicting the
  dominant contemporary view that fermentation was a purely chemical
  decomposition process with no living agent involved. [SHI; WIKI] He held
  this as a minority scientific position at the time he first advanced it.
- 1857: moved to Paris as director of scientific studies at the École
  Normale Supérieure (his own alma mater). [WIKI]
- Fermentation work led directly into public controversy with Félix-Archimède
  Pouchet, a naturalist who argued for "spontaneous generation" — that
  microscopic life could arise spontaneously from non-living matter — using
  his own experiments with hay infusions exposed to air. Pasteur argued
  Pouchet had simply failed to exclude airborne germs/contamination from his
  experiments. [SPRINGER-POUCHET; Encyclopedia.com cross-check]
- January 1864: the Académie des Sciences appointed a commission to judge
  the dispute; per the scholarly record the commission's members were
  friends and defenders of Pasteur — i.e., not a neutral panel.
  [SPRINGER-POUCHET / Encyclopedia.com]
- April 1864: Pasteur gave a set-piece public lecture at the Sorbonne, in
  front of the scientific, political, and social elite of Paris, delivering
  the now-famous line "The spontaneous generation of microscopic beings is a
  mere chimera" and asserting there is "not a single known circumstance" of
  microscopic life arising without parentage. Contemporary and later
  historical assessment of this lecture describes it as "brilliant" but
  "hardly objective" — i.e., a persuasive public performance more than a
  neutral scientific presentation. [SPRINGER-POUCHET/Encyclopedia.com]
- June 1864: Pouchet, apparently recognizing the commission was biased
  toward Pasteur, withdrew from the contest without repeating his
  experiments to the commission's satisfaction — meaning the dispute was
  won more by procedural/rhetorical circumstance than by a fully symmetric
  experimental resolution. [SPRINGER-POUCHET/Encyclopedia.com]
- Historically ironic footnote documented in the scholarly literature: BOTH
  Pasteur and Pouchet were working from the same false shared assumption
  (that boiling water reliably killed all living organisms) — neither
  understood heat-resistant bacterial endospores existed. Pasteur's
  conclusion happened to be correct, but not for fully rigorous reasons by
  modern standards. [SPRINGER-POUCHET/Encyclopedia.com] — relevant to how
  he handled a genuinely uncertain, methodologically imperfect situation:
  he argued from confident public rhetoric rather than acknowledging the
  method's real limitations.
- The famous "swan-neck flask" experiments (sterilized broth in flasks with
  long curved necks that let air in but trapped dust/microbes at the bend)
  date to this general period and were used as further public proof against
  spontaneous generation; Pasteur declared: "Never will the doctrine of
  spontaneous generation recover from the mortal blow of this simple
  experiment." [WIKI, cross-checked]
- 1858–1867: as director of scientific studies at ENS, Pasteur ran the
  institution's discipline in what contemporaries and later historians
  describe as an inflexible, authoritarian manner — dealing "summarily and
  unsympathetically" with student complaints about food and rules; by 1863
  he was "openly appalled" by what he saw as student insubordination. [WIKI,
  independently corroborated via search of ENS institutional history
  sources]
- The "bean/mutton-stew revolt": Pasteur mandated that a mutton stew the
  students disliked be served (and eaten) every Monday, and separately
  threatened expulsion over smoking. Students rebelled; of roughly 80
  students, the large majority (accounts differ slightly on exact numbers,
  in the 70s of 80) resigned rather than comply, with only a handful
  remaining. [WIKI, corroborated] — a documented episode of holding a rigid
  position on a minor disciplinary matter to the point of triggering a mass
  walkout rather than compromising.
- 1867: Pasteur was removed from his ENS administrator/director post
  specifically because of his rigid, unpopular handling of this and related
  student conflicts — both colleagues and students had come to see him as
  too authoritarian. [WIKI, corroborated]
- 1867: also became chair of organic chemistry at the Sorbonne, but later
  resigned this post due to poor health (see Period 5, 1868 stroke). [WIKI]

## Life Period 4 — The Silkworm Disease Investigation (1865–1870)

- 1865: at the direct request of the French government, Pasteur was
  assigned to investigate "pébrine," a disease devastating silkworm farms,
  especially in southern France, and threatening the French silk industry.
  He had essentially no prior background in this specific area of biology —
  he was, up to this point, principally a chemist. [ACAD-SILK; SHI]
- He devoted five full years (1865–1870) to this problem. Historians
  describe this period as a genuine "epistemological rupture" in his own
  intellectual development — the point at which he converted from a chemist
  into what would now be called a biologist. [ACAD-SILK] — a documented
  instance of committing years of sustained effort to a field he had to
  learn essentially from scratch, at government request, rather than
  staying within his established chemistry expertise.
- Discovered the "disease" was actually two separate parasitic infections
  (pébrine and flacherie, later identified as caused by microsporidia and
  bacteria respectively) rather than one. [ACAD-SILK, WIKI]
- Established that the disease spread via contact between diseased and
  healthy worms, via contaminated food, AND via transmission from mother
  moth to offspring through the egg — and developed a practical screening
  method (microscopic examination of moth tissue) to identify and cull
  infected breeding stock, a method still used in the global sericulture
  industry. [ACAD-SILK, WIKI]
- His wife Marie and his surviving daughter assisted directly in this field
  research (see Period 2 note on Marie's role). [Search synthesis]
- Early in the investigation he reportedly believed the visible "corpuscles"
  he observed were symptoms of the disease rather than its actual cause,
  before revising this to identify them as causal — i.e., a documented
  instance of his own working hypothesis being wrong at first and being
  updated as more evidence came in, rather than defended past its useful
  life. [WIKI, general cross-check]

## Life Period 5 — Personal Tragedy, War, and Health Crisis (1865–1871)

- The Pasteurs had five children; three died of typhoid fever during his
  lifetime: 2-year-old Camille (1865), 9-year-old Jeanne (at Arbois), and
  12½-year-old Cécile (23 May 1866). Only two children (Jean Baptiste and
  Marie Louise) survived to adulthood. [WIKI, corroborated via multiple
  independent secondary sources located by search]
- Multiple secondary historical accounts connect the accumulated grief and
  overwork of this period directly to his health collapse: in 1868 Pasteur
  suffered a cerebral hemorrhage (stroke) that permanently paralyzed part of
  his left arm and leg. [SHI; WIKI, corroborated]
- Despite this partial paralysis, he continued working — SHI's biography
  specifically notes he "continued working" after the stroke, though from
  this point on he relied much more heavily on trusted assistants (notably
  Émile Roux and Charles Chamberland) to carry out the physical/manual
  laboratory work he himself directed. [SHI; WIKI]
- 1870–71: Franco-Prussian War. France's defeat and the Prussian army's
  actions provoked an intense, lasting reaction in Pasteur: he vowed to
  inscribe all his subsequent scientific works with "Hatred toward Prussia.
  Revenge! Revenge!" [Search synthesis, cross-checked across multiple
  independent secondary sources]
- 1871: Pasteur returned, in protest, an honorary M.D. that the University
  of Bonn (Germany) had awarded him in 1868 — publishing the exchange of
  angry letters with the Bonn medical faculty dean as a pamphlet, in which
  he denounced the "barbarity" Prussia and its king had inflicted on France.
  [Search synthesis, corroborated across independent sources]
- Despite tempting job offers from Milan and Pisa after the war, Pasteur
  chose to remain in France — reportedly influenced both by his wife's
  reluctance to emigrate and by his own view that leaving France in the wake
  of its defeat would amount to desertion. [Search synthesis, corroborated]
- Even near the end of his life (1895), his wartime resentment had not
  faded: he declined the Prussian Ordre Pour le Mérite. [Search synthesis,
  corroborated]
- After the war, the new French government built Pasteur a dedicated
  laboratory, relieved him of teaching duties, and granted him a pension —
  freeing him to focus entirely on disease research going forward. [SHI]

## Life Period 6 — Germ Theory Consolidated; Chicken Cholera & Anthrax Vaccines (1877–1882)

- Late 1870s: working increasingly on disease-causing microorganisms in
  animals and humans, building directly on the earlier fermentation/
  spontaneous-generation work. [WIKI, general]
- 1879–80: while investigating chicken (fowl) cholera, Pasteur discovered
  vaccine attenuation essentially by accident — cultures of the cholera
  organism that had been left unused/exposed during a vacation period had
  weakened, and when injected into chickens produced immunity rather than
  disease. [SHI] — a documented case of productively noticing and
  capitalizing on an unplanned/accidental experimental outcome rather than
  discarding it as a failed batch.
- Priority dispute over the ANTHRAX vaccine specifically: veterinarian
  Henry (Jean-Joseph-Henri) Toussaint had developed an anthrax immunization
  approach using a heat-treatment method before Pasteur's own public
  triumph. Pasteur is documented as having contested/downplayed Toussaint's
  priority, "on grounds of jealousy" per the historical record, while
  ultimately claiming public credit through his own large-scale public
  demonstration. [WIKI, corroborated]
- May 1881, Pouilly-le-Fort (near Melun): veterinarian Hippolyte Rossignol —
  himself a skeptic who rejected germ theory — publicly challenged Pasteur
  to prove the anthrax vaccine worked in an open field trial. Pasteur
  accepted. Rossignol himself was given a substantive role: he selected the
  animals, conducted observations, and supervised the final exposure to
  virulent anthrax — i.e., Pasteur deliberately built his chief public
  doubter into the trial's own oversight structure rather than avoiding him.
  [GAVI]
- Protocol: 24 sheep + 1 goat + 6 cows vaccinated twice (5 and 17 May); a
  control group of 24 sheep + 1 goat + 4 cows left unvaccinated; all animals
  (both groups) challenged with virulent anthrax bacilli on 31 May; results
  observed 2 June before a reassembled public crowd. Result: all 25
  unvaccinated sheep died; all 25 vaccinated sheep survived in good health.
  [GAVI, cross-checked against search summary of the primary "Summary
  Report" document]
- On the day itself, Pasteur is documented as having addressed the large
  public crowd — which included politicians, journalists, and farmers — in
  "a lay and familiar tone," successfully conveying the trial's stakes to a
  non-expert audience; a contemporary/historical assessment specifically
  states "Pasteur charmed his audience." [GAVI] — direct evidence of
  comfort and skill performing under public, high-stakes scrutiny rather
  than avoiding exposure.
- The major documented ethical complication (per Geison's notebook
  research, corroborated by GAVI and HOV): the vaccine actually used in the
  public trial was NOT Pasteur's own live-attenuated (oxygen-exposure)
  method as publicly implied — Pasteur's own attenuation technique had
  reportedly not produced a reliable vaccine. Instead, the trial used a
  chemically inactivated preparation (potassium bichromate/dichromate),
  originally pioneered by rival Toussaint and refined by Pasteur's own
  assistant Charles Chamberland. Pasteur ordered Chamberland and colleagues
  to keep this substitution quiet, and never publicly disclosed the
  chemical-inactivation method's true role — the discrepancy only became
  known when Pasteur's private notebooks were finally published in the
  1970s. [GEISON; GAVI; HOV — all three converge on this same episode] —
  this is one of the two most load-bearing "behavior under pressure/stakes,
  with a live public reputation on the line" episodes in the whole record.
- Despite this behind-the-scenes substitution, when France sought to honor
  Pasteur personally with the Legion of Honor for the anthrax success, he
  insisted his assistants Roux and Chamberland also be honored — he took
  the higher "blue ribbon" grade himself, but ensured his collaborators
  received the "red" grade rather than accepting sole individual credit in
  that specific honors decision. [Search synthesis on Pasteur's
  collaborators, corroborated] — notable tension with the anthrax
  concealment episode above: he was willing to share formal/public credit
  with subordinates in one context while concealing their actual technical
  contribution in another.
- 1881: at the International Medical Congress, Pasteur's rivalry with the
  younger German bacteriologist Robert Koch became openly hostile —
  intensified by post-Franco-Prussian-War national tension and by a
  mistranslation of a term in Pasteur's own lecture from French to German,
  which triggered Koch's anger. Two of Koch's bacteriologist colleagues then
  published a denunciation of the entirety of Pasteur's anthrax research
  dating back to 1877. Koch specifically attacked Pasteur's methodological
  rigor, saying Pasteur was not qualified to study anthrax because he could
  not grow it in pure culture, and calling his experiments worthless.
  Pasteur is documented as replying dismissively to these attacks in his own
  subsequent speeches. [Search synthesis of multiple corroborating
  historical/academic sources — Cell.com, ResearchGate, HandWiki] — Pasteur
  is on record as engaging combatively/dismissively in this dispute rather
  than disengaging or conceding methodological points.
- Separately, Pasteur carried a long-running, lifelong priority dispute with
  chemist/physician Antoine Béchamp over who had really discovered core
  aspects of the fermentation/germ findings; per the historical record,
  Béchamp had done earlier preliminary work but Pasteur's account came to
  dominate the historical record regardless. [WIKI, cross-checked] — a
  second, separate long-duration priority conflict (distinct from Toussaint
  and from Koch), suggesting a pattern rather than an isolated incident.

## Life Period 7 — The Rabies Vaccine & Founding the Pasteur Institute (1882–1888)

- Pasteur turned to rabies (which he privately called "the invisible
  dragon") as his next major target after the anthrax success. [IP-HIST]
- Development involved substantial animal testing (rabbits, dogs) over
  several years before any human application was contemplated. [IP-RABIES,
  general]
- 6 July 1885: nine-year-old Joseph Meister, bitten fourteen times by a
  rabid dog two days earlier, was brought to Pasteur. Facing what was
  regarded as near-certain death from rabies without intervention, Pasteur
  administered his experimental rabies treatment — thirteen injections of
  progressively more potent rabid-spinal-cord material over roughly ten
  days. [IP-HIST; IP-RABIES]
- Pasteur's own documented words on the decision: "The child's death
  appeared inevitable. I decided not without acute and harrowing anxiety, as
  may be imagined, to apply to Joseph Meister the method which I had found
  consistently successful with dogs." [Search synthesis, direct quotation
  corroborated across multiple independent sources] — first-person evidence
  of real emotional distress/anxiety at the point of an irreversible,
  high-stakes decision, not a clinically detached account.
- Crucially, Pasteur did NOT make this decision alone or immediately: his
  own close collaborators, including Émile Roux, initially OPPOSED giving
  the vaccine to a human, since it had at that point only been tested
  successfully in dogs and rabbits — Roux is specifically documented as
  refusing to participate in the Meister vaccination, likely because he
  considered it unjust/premature. It was a second physician, Dr.
  Jacques-Joseph Grancher, who ultimately persuaded Pasteur to proceed,
  and it was Grancher (a licensed physician, which Pasteur himself was not)
  who administered/oversaw the actual injections. Pasteur also separately
  consulted physicians Alfred Vulpian and Grancher before proceeding. [HOV;
  IP-RABIES; WIKI, cross-checked] — a genuine group deliberation with real,
  documented internal disagreement (his own most trusted collaborator was
  against it), not a unilateral decision Pasteur simply imposed.
- Pasteur himself held no medical license, meaning treating Meister directly
  carried a real legal/professional risk of prosecution, independent of the
  scientific risk to the child. [WIKI, corroborated]
- Documented ethical complications from Geison's notebook research (per HOV):
  Pasteur's public claims about how extensively the rabies method had been
  validated in animals before Meister did not match his own private
  notebooks — he had NOT in fact tested it on "dozens" of rabid dogs with
  uniformly good results as he later stated; contemporaneous notebooks
  reportedly show a much smaller number of successful prior trials (~11
  cited in one secondary account) than the number he later publicly claimed
  (~50). Separately, at least two other human patients beyond Meister
  reportedly received rabies treatment around this period with worse
  outcomes documented in the notebooks: an adult man (about 60) who received
  one injection and then abandoned treatment, and a young girl who died
  after a single injection following a dog bite. [HOV, GEISON via search
  synthesis] — again the same pattern as the anthrax episode: real
  documented uncertainty/imperfect results managed via a more polished,
  more certain-sounding public narrative than the private record supports.
- Meister recovered and did not develop rabies — the first successful human
  rabies vaccination. News of the success spread internationally and
  triggered a rapid influx of bite-victim patients seeking treatment at
  Pasteur's laboratory. [IP-HIST]
- By 1886, roughly 350 people had been treated with the rabies method, with
  only a single documented rabies case among them (i.e., the method held up
  under a much larger and less controlled real-world patient population than
  the original single case). [WIKI, corroborated]
- The surge in demand for rabies treatment directly motivated founding a
  dedicated institution: the Institut Pasteur received official charitable
  status by government decree on 4 June 1887 and opened 14 November 1888,
  funded via Pasteur's own successful international public fundraising
  appeal. [IP-HIST]
- Pasteur explicitly framed the new institute's mission in humanitarian,
  universalist terms rather than nationalist ones (despite his own personal
  wartime nationalism documented in Period 5): "You don't ask someone who is
  suffering, 'What country are you from?' You tell them: 'It's enough that
  you are suffering – you're in my hands and I will ease that suffering.'"
  [IP-HIST, direct quotation]

## Life Period 8 — Institute Leadership & Late Life (1888–1895)

- As director of the new institute, Pasteur is documented as having
  welcomed a genuine intellectual rival/complement onto his own staff:
  he invited immunologist Élie Metchnikoff (who had his own, differing
  theory of immune response — phagocytosis) to join and develop his ideas
  at the institute, rather than treating him as competition to exclude.
  [SHI] — notably different in character from the Koch/Toussaint/Béchamp
  conflicts in Period 6.
- His core inner circle of collaborators at the institute is referred to in
  the historical literature as the "five musketeers of Louis Pasteur":
  Émile Duclaux, Charles Chamberland, Émile Roux, Jacques-Joseph Grancher,
  and Élie Metchnikoff — each became a substantial, independent scientific
  figure in his own right while working under/alongside Pasteur, rather than
  remaining purely subordinate technicians. Roux in particular is
  specifically documented as a genuine intellectual collaborator who "did
  not always agree with" Pasteur, not merely an obedient assistant. [Search
  synthesis of the Institut-Pasteur-affiliated scholarly article on these
  five colleagues]
- Continuing secrecy about his own working process: in 1878 (retroactively
  relevant here since it defined institute-era practice) Pasteur had already
  instructed his family that his private laboratory notebooks should never
  be shown to anyone; multiple secondary accounts (via GEISON) also describe
  him as not permitting assistants to keep independent notes of their own,
  maintaining a personal monopoly over the documented research record. His
  notebooks stayed within his family until 1964 and were not fully released
  to scholars until the 1970s. [GEISON via search synthesis, corroborated]
- Died 28 September 1895 (aged 72) in Marnes-la-Coquette, France; interred
  in a vault beneath the Institut Pasteur rather than a conventional
  cemetery. [WIKI]
- Received numerous honors across his career, reflecting sustained
  contemporaneous recognition rather than only posthumous reputation:
  Chevalier of the Legion of Honour (1853) with an associated cash prize
  from the Pharmaceutical Society; Rumford Medal, Royal Society of London
  (1856); multiple French Academy prizes (1859–62); Copley Medal (1874);
  Grand Cross of the Legion of Honour and election to the Académie française
  (1881); Albert Medal (1882); Cameron Prize for Therapeutics (1889);
  Leeuwenhoek Medal (1895). [WIKI, cross-checked]

### Key achievements
1. **Discovery of molecular chirality/asymmetry (1848).** By painstakingly
   hand-separating two mirror-image forms of tartrate crystals under a
   microscope, the young Pasteur founded the field of stereochemistry —
   showing that identical molecules can exist in non-superimposable
   mirror-image forms. [SHI]
2. **Disproof of spontaneous generation & establishment of germ theory
   (1859–64).** Through the swan-neck flask experiments and a decisive
   public victory over Pouchet at the Académie des Sciences, Pasteur helped
   establish that microorganisms come only from other microorganisms, not
   spontaneously from non-living matter — a foundational premise of modern
   microbiology and medicine. [SPRINGER-POUCHET; WIKI]
3. **The anthrax vaccine and the Pouilly-le-Fort public trial (1881).** A
   dramatic, deliberately public open-field experiment — with his chief
   skeptic built into its own oversight — that convinced the scientific and
   agricultural world that vaccination against bacterial disease was
   possible and effective, saving substantial livestock industry losses.
   [GAVI]
4. **The rabies vaccine and the first successful human treatment (1885) /
   founding of the Institut Pasteur (1887–88).** Treating nine-year-old
   Joseph Meister launched the modern era of applied human vaccination and
   directly led to founding an institution that, as of 2014, had produced
   ten Nobel laureates. [IP-HIST; IP-RABIES]

### Revealing anecdotes
1. **The father who wanted a teacher, not an artist.** As a teenager Pasteur
   produced accomplished pastel portraits of his family and neighbors, later
   preserved in the Institut Pasteur's own museum — but his tanner father
   saw the talent as a distraction from the more secure goal of a teaching
   career, a tension between artistic and "practical" inclination in the
   household that predates his scientific career entirely. [WIKI,
   corroborated via search synthesis]
2. **The mutton-stew mutiny.** As director of scientific studies at the
   École Normale Supérieure, Pasteur's insistence that students eat a
   mandatory mutton stew every Monday (plus threatened expulsion over
   smoking) triggered a mass resignation of the vast majority of the
   student body rather than backing down — and ultimately cost him the
   position in 1867. A small, almost comic disciplinary flashpoint that
   nonetheless had real career consequences. [WIKI, corroborated]
3. **Building his own chief skeptic into the biggest trial of his career.**
   At Pouilly-le-Fort, rather than avoiding the veterinarian Hippolyte
   Rossignol who had publicly doubted both him and germ theory generally,
   Pasteur let Rossignol select the animals and personally supervise the
   final anthrax exposure — a calculated bet that a fair, adversarial-witness
   structure would make the result more credible if it succeeded. [GAVI]
4. **"I decided not without acute and harrowing anxiety."** Pasteur's own
   words about the moment he chose to treat Joseph Meister — first-person
   evidence that the decision that made him a global hero was, to him in
   the moment, agonizing rather than confident, and one his own closest
   collaborator (Roux) initially refused to be part of. [HOV; search
   synthesis of the direct quotation]

### Turning points
1. **Failing the ENS entrance exam, then re-preparing and passing with
   distinction (1842–44).** An early, concrete instance of an initial
   failure being met with additional targeted effort rather than
   redirection or resignation — arguably the template for his later
   multi-year commitments to unfamiliar problems (silkworm disease, rabies).
   [WIKI, corroborated]
2. **The 1865–66 deaths of three of his five children from typhoid,
   followed by his own 1868 stroke.** Multiple sources connect this period
   of severe personal loss and physical breakdown directly to his later,
   near-total redirection of his remaining working life toward disease and
   public health research — from a scientist who had been working chiefly
   on crystallography and industrial fermentation toward one consumed by
   the study and defeat of infectious disease. [WIKI; SHI, corroborated]
3. **The Franco-Prussian War (1870–71).** Beyond its emotional impact
   (the returned Bonn degree, the "Revenge!" vow, declining a Prussian
   honor even on his deathbed), the war's aftermath materially changed his
   working conditions: the French state built him a dedicated laboratory,
   removed his teaching obligations, and granted him a pension specifically
   so he could commit to disease research — the practical foundation that
   made the anthrax and rabies work of the 1880s possible. [SHI]

## Documentation gaps (for the record)

- Full original text of Geison's *The Private Science of Louis Pasteur* and
  Debré's *Louis Pasteur* was not directly accessible (paywalled/
  monograph-only) — both were engaged through multiple independent,
  mutually corroborating secondary summaries rather than primary full-text
  reading. The specific factual claims used here (the anthrax substitution,
  the rabies pre-clinical-testing discrepancy, the notebook-secrecy
  instruction) are each corroborated across at least two-to-three
  independent secondary sources, not resting on any single account.
- Detailed day-to-day emotional/interior material is thin for the
  1848–1865 middle career period specifically (crystallography through
  early fermentation work) compared to the richly documented anthrax/rabies
  episodes of the 1880s — the notebook-based revisionist scholarship
  (Geison) concentrates heavily on the 1880s vaccine period, so earlier-career
  behavioral material is more institutional/factual than behavioral.
  Anecdotal source variability was also seen for very a few very
  specific numeric details (e.g., exact number of ENS students who resigned
  during the "bean revolt" — sources given as approximately 70+ of 80,
  figures not perfectly consistent across secondary accounts) — treated as
  approximate rather than exact for that reason.
