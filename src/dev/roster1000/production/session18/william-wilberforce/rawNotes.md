## Period 1: Wealthy youth, Cambridge, early politics, pre-conversion
(1759 – 1784/85)

- Born 24 August 1759 at Wilberforce House, Hull, into a prosperous
  merchant family. [REGENCY, WHM]
- Grandfather (also William Wilberforce) acquired Wilberforce House in
  1732 after marrying into the merchant Thornton family (1711 marriage
  to Sarah Thornton). Family wealth was established merchant-trading
  wealth, not aristocratic. [WHM]
- Father Robert Wilberforce died in 1768 when William was 9. He was sent
  to live in London with his aunt and uncle, Hannah and William
  Wilberforce, who exposed him to Methodist religious influence. His
  mother's family, alarmed at this, intervened and had him brought back
  from London, temporarily suppressing the evangelical leanings that
  would later re-emerge. [REGENCY]
- Attended St John's College, Cambridge (matriculated 1776, BA 1781, MA
  1788). Contemporary accounts describe him at Cambridge as witty, a
  popular singer/conversationalist, and — before his conversion — living
  a socially dissolute undergraduate life (gambling, late nights,
  general high living), a lifestyle later sources contrast pointedly
  with his post-1785 austerity. [REGENCY, HAGUE via reviews]
- At Cambridge he formed a close, lasting friendship with William Pitt
  the Younger — the two entered Parliament within a year of each other
  (Wilberforce 1780, Pitt January 1781) and remained close political
  allies and friends for decades. [REGENCY]
- 1780: elected MP for Hull at age 21. His considerable personal wealth
  (inherited via his merchant family, further increased by an uncle's
  legacy) meant he could contest and win the seat without needing a
  patron — financed his own campaign directly. [HOP, CORROB]
- 25 March 1784: at a mass public meeting of Yorkshire freeholders
  (called to draft a thank-you address to the King for dismissing the
  Fox-North Coalition), Wilberforce was mid-speech when a note from Pitt
  was brought to him announcing Parliament's dissolution that day — he
  relayed the news to the assembled crowd himself, live, from the
  platform. [HOP]
- Immediately afterward, at a gathering of Pitt's supporters, Wilberforce
  and Henry Duncombe were unanimously adopted as the Pittite candidates
  for the county of Yorkshire — then the largest, most populous
  constituency in England. The opposing Coalition candidates withdrew
  before the poll, so Wilberforce was returned essentially without a
  contested election or personal expense, despite Yorkshire elections
  otherwise being notoriously expensive. [HOP]

## Period 2: Evangelical conversion and the decision to stay in politics;
early abolition campaign years, first defeats (1784/85 – ~1791)

- 1784-85: while travelling in Europe with Isaac Milner (a Cambridge
  contemporary), Wilberforce underwent a profound religious/evangelical
  conversion experience — described in his own later diary entries as a
  period of acute guilt and self-reproach over how he had spent his life
  and privileges. Quoted diary passage (originating in SONS, quoted
  widely in secondary literature): "The deep guilt of my past life
  forced itself upon me in the strongest colours, and I [stand]
  condemned for having wasted my precious time, and opportunities, and
  talents." [SONS/via secondary quotation, HAGUE via reviews]
- Following the conversion, Wilberforce seriously considered abandoning
  political life altogether to enter the clergy — a real, weighed
  decision, not a passing thought. [REGENCY, HAGUE via reviews]
- 2 December 1785: wrote to the evangelical minister John Newton (former
  slave-ship captain turned clergyman, author of "Amazing Grace")
  requesting a private meeting for spiritual guidance, explicitly
  agonizing in the letter over whether to reveal his changed convictions
  to anyone: "I wish to have some serious conversation with you. ... I
  have had ten thousand doubts within myself, whether or not I should
  discover myself to you; but every argument against it has its
  foundation in pride." [SONS/via secondary quotation]
- Newton met with him, encouraged his faith, and specifically urged him
  NOT to withdraw from public life — advice Wilberforce ultimately took.
  After the meeting Wilberforce recorded in his diary: "When I came
  away, my mind was in a calm and tranquil state, more humbled, looking
  more devoutly up to God." Both Newton and Pitt separately counseled
  him to remain in politics rather than take orders. [SONS/via secondary
  quotation, REGENCY]
- 28 October 1787: diary entry framing his life's dual mission — "God
  Almighty has set before me two great objects, the suppression of the
  Slave Trade and the Reformation of Manners" (by which he meant moral/
  social reform of Britain generally, not merely etiquette). This
  single diary line is treated by multiple sources as the founding
  articulation of the rest of his public career. [SONS/via secondary
  quotation, CORROB]
- Following a 1787 Royal Proclamation (which Wilberforce himself had
  urged George III to issue) "for the Discouragement of Vice," he worked
  toward founding what became the Society for the Suppression of Vice
  (formally established 1802 as successor to the earlier Society for
  Reformation of Manners) — targeting Sabbath-breaking, obscene
  publications, false weights and measures, disorderly houses, cruelty
  to animals, and illegal gambling. [CORROB]
- 1789: introduced twelve resolutions against the slave trade in the
  Commons; Pitt supported them, but they were not enacted and the matter
  was postponed rather than decided. [HOP, CORROB]
- 1791: brought his first formal Abolition of the Slave Trade bill to a
  vote. It was defeated 163 to 88, in a political climate made fearful
  by the ongoing French Revolution and recent slave revolts in the
  French West Indies (many conservative MPs also held direct financial
  investments in the slave trade). Rather than withdrawing, Wilberforce
  responded with a public commitment that would define the rest of his
  public life: "Never, never will we desist till we have wiped away
  this scandal from the Christian name." [CORROB — vote count and quote
  corroborated across multiple independent secondary accounts]
- The 1791 defeat had a mobilizing public effect independent of
  Parliament: hundreds of thousands of Britons signed abolition
  petitions and an estimated 400,000 joined a nationwide boycott of
  slave-produced West Indian sugar — evidence of the extra-parliamentary
  organizing dimension of the campaign running alongside Wilberforce's
  own parliamentary efforts. [CORROB]

## Period 3: The long middle campaign — repeated parliamentary defeat,
personal health crisis, marriage, Clapham Sect organizing, eventual 1807
victory (1792 – 1807)

- 1792: a compromise "gradual abolition" measure (backed by Home
  Secretary Henry Dundas, opposed by Wilberforce's own preference for
  immediate abolition) passed the Commons instead of Wilberforce's
  original immediate-abolition bill — a real tactical defeat/disappointment,
  not a win reframed as one. [CORROB]
- Wilberforce continued introducing abolition bills essentially every
  year or two through the 1790s regardless of outcome: 1793, 1797, 1798,
  1799. The 3 April 1798 bill was defeated by a very narrow margin, 87
  votes to 83. [CORROB]
- 1788 (age 29): began taking opium daily on his doctor's recommendation,
  almost certainly to manage ulcerative colitis (severe abdominal pain,
  recurrent ulceration, debilitating flare-ups that required travel to
  Bath for the medicinal waters). This is a chronic condition that ran
  alongside essentially his entire political career from this point
  forward. [SOJO]
- Opium dosage escalated over decades: 5 grains/day by 1796, rising to
  12 grains/day later, with increases in cold weather or during other
  illness. His diaries record withdrawal effects when he missed his
  evening dose: he would wake "forced to lie in bed, great sneezing and
  other signs of spasm." [SOJO]
- His diary also records the drug's apparent effect on his mental state
  during difficult periods, e.g.: "Corrupt imaginations are perpetually
  rising in my mind and innumerable fears close me in on every side."
  Contemporary observers noted he grew "more untidy, indolent... and
  absent-minded" over the years, and his eyesight declined — attributed
  in later biographical work (John Pollock's research, cited by SOJO) to
  slow opium poisoning. Pollock's research also found Wilberforce
  attempted to quit the drug multiple times without lasting success.
  [SOJO]
- Despite the chronic illness, he sustained an unusually demanding
  double life for decades: full parliamentary/campaign work plus a
  four-years-long personal writing project. He wrote *A Practical View
  of the Prevailing Religious System of Professed Christians... Contrasted
  with Real Christianity* over four years, publishing it in 1797; it sold
  7,500 copies in its first six months, a bestseller by the standards of
  the day, and became personally revealing/autobiographical in its mix
  of rational argument and personal testimony about the difference
  between nominal and genuine faith. [CORROB]
- 30 May 1797: married Barbara Ann Spooner in Bath. They had six
  children (William, Barbara, Elizabeth, Robert, Samuel, Henry); both
  daughters predeceased him. [REGENCY]
- His eldest son, William, later caused him significant financial
  distress; his two younger sons (Robert, Samuel — the future
  biographers) pursued clerical careers instead. [REGENCY] Corroborated
  independently: in 1830 Wilberforce sold the family's Hull house
  (Wilberforce House itself) specifically to pay off his son's debts.
  [WHM]
- 1792-1797: lived at Battersea Rise, the Clapham home of his cousin
  Henry Thornton (a wealthy banker who had undergone a similar
  evangelical awakening) — the house's purpose-built library became the
  central meeting place of what became known as the Clapham Sect, a
  network of roughly a dozen interconnected evangelical families
  (Thornton, Zachary Macaulay, James Stephen, Lord Teignmouth, Charles
  Grant, Charles Simeon, John Venn, and others, several linked further
  by intermarriage, e.g. Babington married Macaulay's sister).
  Wilberforce was its acknowledged parliamentary leader (the group was
  also nicknamed "the Saints"). This is documented collaborative,
  cohabiting organizing behavior sustained over years, not a loose
  political alliance. [CORROB]
- His working partnership with Thomas Clarkson (who was not an MP) shows
  a clear, sustained division of labor: Clarkson traveled the country
  gathering firsthand evidence (an estimated 35,000 miles logged on
  horseback, interviewing sailors, surgeons, and slave-ship crew),
  feeding that evidence directly to Wilberforce for use in Parliament,
  while Wilberforce supplied the parliamentary platform and set-piece
  speeches Clarkson himself as a non-MP could not deliver. Clarkson is
  on record acknowledging the necessity of Wilberforce's role: "What
  could the committee have done without the parliamentary aid of Mr
  Wilberforce?" [CORROB]
- Real conflict/credit dispute, not merely a footnote: after Wilberforce's
  death, his sons' 1838 biography (SONS) was seen by Clarkson as
  understating his own contribution to the campaign; Clarkson responded
  in the same year with a pamphlet, "Strictures on a Life of William
  Wilberforce," publicly disputing their account. This is a genuine,
  documented interpersonal/legacy conflict within the abolitionist
  circle, occurring after Wilberforce's death but reflecting real
  tension over credit that existed between the two men's camps. [SONS
  bibliographic description]
- Wilberforce's life was directly threatened at points during the
  campaign, particularly after he publicly criticized a named slave-ship
  captain (Robert Norris) in Parliament; he faced not only physical-safety
  concerns but the loss of friendships and sustained political pressure
  to abandon the cause. [CORROB]
- He was the target of sustained, personalized published criticism: a
  pointed attack from journalist/politician William Cobbett accused him
  of hypocrisy for focusing on distant slaves while ignoring the
  conditions of "free British labourers" at home; he was also separately
  denounced in pamphlets by Unitarian writers, and later (1824) by
  planter-turned-editor James MacQueen, who accused Wilberforce and
  Clarkson of "dreadful misrepresentations" about West Indian planters.
  [CORROB]
- Throughout his career he deliberately maintained a position as a
  financially-independent MP who belonged to no party — by his own later
  account to his sons, he resolved from early in his career "to attach
  himself to no party, but to be independent." He worked with both Tory
  and Whig governments depending on the issue, was never offered and
  never sought cabinet office, and his private financial means (rather
  than any salaried post) were what made this independence practically
  possible. [HOP, CORROB]
- 1804: reintroduced an abolition bill; it passed the Commons but was
  rejected by the House of Lords. 1805: another bill was defeated in the
  Commons by a narrow margin — notably, this time even Pitt (his oldest,
  closest political friend, and by-then Prime Minister again) did not
  actively support it, evidence of some real strain or at least
  divergence between the two men's political priorities on the issue
  even within a lasting friendship. [CORROB]
- After Pitt's death in 1806, the new Grenville/Fox government made
  abolition a governmental priority instead of a matter of personal
  sympathy. In May 1806, working with this new government, abolitionists
  secured passage of the Foreign Slave Trade Act — a tactically
  incremental measure that cut off roughly three-quarters of the trade
  (the portion not directly tied to the British West Indies) without
  yet abolishing the whole trade — a deliberate partial-win strategy
  rather than an all-or-nothing approach. [CORROB]
- 23 February 1807: the decisive vote on the Abolition of the Slave
  Trade bill passed 283 to 16. On the vote, the House of Commons rose
  and cheered Wilberforce directly; he, in turn, bowed his head and wept
  openly at the result, after roughly two decades of annual/biennial
  parliamentary defeat on the same cause. The bill received royal assent
  on 25 March 1807. [CORROB — widely and consistently reported]

## Period 4: Post-abolition-of-the-trade to death — continued
emancipation pressure, declining health, final victory, death (1807 –
1833)

- The 1807 Act abolished the trade in slaves but not slavery itself
  within the British Empire — Wilberforce continued campaigning for full
  emancipation for the remaining 26 years of his life, a second,
  even-longer phase of the same core cause. He authored further
  published appeals to sustain public attention during this period
  (referenced in secondary bibliographic material as "A Letter on the
  Abolition of the Slave Trade" and a subsequent "Appeal," part of his
  1807-1823 activity) — noted here as documented but not independently
  verified in full text this session. [HOP, weakly corroborated — flagged
  as a documentation gap below]
- 1824: one of 22 reformers who founded the Society for the Prevention
  of Cruelty to Animals (later Royal — RSPCA) at a meeting at Old
  Slaughter's Coffee House, alongside Richard Martin MP and Rev. Arthur
  Broome — evidence of sustained organizing energy directed at a second,
  distinct social-reform cause late in life, alongside the ongoing
  emancipation campaign. Over his life he was involved in founding,
  funding, or leading over sixty philanthropic/reform organizations
  altogether (child labor, smallpox inoculation, education of the deaf,
  among others). [CORROB]
- Declining health (the chronic bowel condition, opium dependence, poor
  eyesight, and general physical decline) progressively limited his
  capacity for the most demanding parliamentary work in his later years;
  he formally retired from Parliament in 1825. [REGENCY]
- 26 July 1833: while on his deathbed, his friend Thomas Babington
  Macaulay brought him the news that the Slavery Abolition Bill —
  granting freedom to all enslaved people within the British Empire —
  had passed Parliament. His recorded response: "Thank God that I should
  have lived to witness the day in which England is willing to give
  [twenty million pounds] sterling for the abolition of slavery." [CORROB
  — widely and consistently reported]
- Died 29 July 1833, three days after receiving that news — living just
  long enough to know the cause he had pursued since roughly 1787 had
  finally succeeded in full, but not living to see the Act itself take
  effect (it received royal assent 29 August 1833 and came into force a
  year later). [CORROB, REGENCY]
- Buried at Westminster Abbey; his funeral drew large public attendance,
  reflecting the scale of his public standing at death. [REGENCY]

  further diary-level emotional/introspective material in a future
  research pass.
- The History of Parliament Online page itself could not be directly
  fetched (401/403); everything attributed to HOP here is via detailed
  search-engine excerpts that quote the page's own text, which I judge
  reliable but is a weaker access method than direct reading.
- The 1807-1823 "sustaining the campaign toward full emancipation"
  period is the thinnest-documented stretch in this research pass — I
  could not directly access a detailed academic account of exactly how
  he organized or what setbacks he faced in these specific years beyond
  the general framing that he continued campaigning and publishing. A
  future pass should specifically target this period (e.g. via Hague's
  or Pollock's full biographies, or Wilberforce's own 1807 "Letter" and
  1823 "Appeal" pamphlets) for the mid-career episodes it likely contains
  (further specific parliamentary setbacks, register-of-slaves bill
  fights, his role advising the next generation of campaigners like
  Thomas Fowell Buxton, who took over parliamentary leadership of the

- I found no documented evidence in the sources I could access this
  session of specific accounts of Wilberforce in personal conflict with
  named collaborators (i.e., Clapham Sect infighting) — the one
  documented interpersonal conflict found is the posthumous
  Clarkson/sons'-biography credit dispute, which is real but occurred
  after his death, not during his lifetime as a live behavioral episode.
  This may be a real gap (no living-relationship conflict on record) or
  may simply reflect the limits of this pass's source access — flagged,
  not assumed either way.
- I did not find primary-sourced detail (this session) on his exact
  day-to-day working habits/schedule (e.g. how he structured research
  and speech preparation, whether he delegated staff work, his reading
  habits) beyond the general "detailed diary-keeping as self-monitoring"

## Public-facing content assets

*(Separate from the evidence above — for future editorial content, not
scoring. All citations as above.)*

**Key achievements:**
1. Principal parliamentary leader of the campaign that produced the
   Abolition of the Slave Trade Act 1807, after roughly 20 years of
   sustained effort and repeated legislative defeat (1791, 1792, 1793,
   1797, 1798, 1799, 1804, 1805 all failed or were watered down before
   1807 succeeded 283-16). [CORROB]
2. Lived to see the Slavery Abolition Act pass in 1833, days before his
   own death — completing, in his own lifetime, the second and larger
   goal (full emancipation, not just ending the trade) that the 1807 Act
   had left unfinished. [CORROB]
3. Co-founded the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals in
   1824 (now the RSPCA, the world's oldest animal-welfare charity) and
   was involved in founding or leading over sixty reform/philanthropic
   organizations across his lifetime. [CORROB]
4. Authored *A Practical View of... Real Christianity* (1797), a
   bestselling and influential religious work that helped popularize
   evangelical Christianity among Britain's upper and middle classes.
   [CORROB]

**Revealing anecdotes:**
1. At a mass political meeting in March 1784, mid-speech, he was handed
   a note announcing Parliament's dissolution — and simply relayed the
   news to the crowd himself on the spot, before being adopted as a
   candidate for Yorkshire (the largest constituency in England) within
   the hour. [HOP]
2. After his first great parliamentary defeat in 1791 (163-88), rather
   than stepping back he stood up and pledged never to desist "till we
   have wiped away this scandal from the Christian name" — a promise he
   kept literally, reintroducing the cause in nearly every subsequent
   session for another 16 years. [CORROB]
3. When the decisive 1807 vote finally passed 283-16, the House of
   Commons rose and cheered him directly — and he, after two decades of
   defeat, bowed his head and wept in front of the chamber. [CORROB]
4. On his own deathbed in July 1833, told that the Slavery Abolition
   Bill had passed, his recorded reply was gratitude that he had lived
   to see it — he died three days later, having witnessed but not quite
   outlived the completion of his life's central cause. [CORROB]

**Turning points:**
1. The 1784-85 evangelical conversion (during European travel with Isaac
   Milner), followed by his deliberate, advised decision (via John
   Newton and Pitt) to remain in politics rather than take religious
   orders — the hinge point that redirected an already-successful young
   politician toward a specific moral crusade. [SONS via secondary
   quotation, REGENCY]
2. The 28 October 1787 diary entry naming "the suppression of the Slave
   Trade and the Reformation of Manners" as his two life objects — the
   moment his general evangelical conviction crystallized into a
   specific, named, lifelong dual mission. [SONS via secondary
   quotation]
3. Pitt's death in 1806 and the change of government to Grenville/Fox,
   which shifted abolition from a personal cause with inconsistent
   prime-ministerial backing to an active governmental priority — the
   external political turning point that, combined with two decades of
   Wilberforce's and Clarkson's sustained groundwork, finally produced
   the 1807 victory the following year. [CORROB]
