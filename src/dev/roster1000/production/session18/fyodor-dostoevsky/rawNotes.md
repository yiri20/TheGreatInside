## Life Period 1 — Childhood, family, education (1821–1843)

- Born October 30 (O.S.)/November 11, 1821, in Moscow, at the Mariinsky
  Hospital for the poor, where his father Mikhail Andreevich Dostoevsky
  worked as a staff physician; the family lived on hospital grounds among
  the urban poor from his earliest years. [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO]
- Second of seven children. Described his own childhood, in later
  writings/letters, as "difficult and joyless." [ENCYC]
- His mother, Maria Fyodorovna, was devout and took him on annual
  pilgrimages to monasteries as a boy — an early, direct source of the
  religious formation that resurfaces throughout his adult life and later
  ideological turn. [YALE-BIO]
- His father is independently described (by Yale's synthesis) as
  "tyrannical and domineering" — a character trait his son later explored
  through overbearing-father figures in his fiction (most explicitly
  Fyodor Karamazov in his final novel). [YALE-BIO] [ENCYC]
- In 1828 his father was granted hereditary nobility and purchased a small
  country estate, Darovoe, giving the family (in theory) rural land and
  serfs alongside urban hospital life. [ENCYC]
- His mother died of tuberculosis in 1837, when he was 15–16. His father
  sent him and his brother Mikhail to St. Petersburg the same year to enter
  the Academy for Military Engineers — a career path Dostoevsky did not
  choose for himself and found personally uncongenial. [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO]
- En route to St. Petersburg, as a teenager, he witnessed a courier
  brutally beat a coachman, who in turn whipped the horses — a specific
  scene of casual cruelty/violence he retained and reused decades later in
  both *Notes from Underground* and *Crime and Punishment*, indicating a
  long memory for morally charged observed events and a habit of converting
  raw lived experience directly into literary material. [ENCYC]
- On June 6, 1839, his father died suddenly at the Darovoe estate, away
  from Fyodor (who was at the academy in St. Petersburg). The cause is
  genuinely disputed across sources: (a) his brother Andrey Dostoevsky's
  account claims the father's serfs murdered him after he lost his temper
  and shouted at them, and that police were bribed to record a false
  cause; (b) the alternative/more medically likely explanation offered by
  later scholarship is a stroke/apoplexy; a third folk account blames
  alcoholism following the wife's death. No definitive resolution exists in
  the sources reviewed. [ENCYC] [SEARCH-AGG]
- At the Military Engineering Academy he was, by his own account in
  letters, not a brilliant or dedicated student, and instead was
  "passionately devoted" to literature — particularly Schiller and Homer —
  even while formally training for a technical/military career; he
  compared the *Iliad*'s vision of harmony to Christ's, a comparison he
  returned to decades later in *The Brothers Karamazov*. This is early,
  direct evidence of sustained intellectual interest diverging from his
  assigned institutional path. [ENCYC]
- Graduated the Academy in 1843, served briefly as a military engineer,
  then voluntarily retired from military service in 1844 specifically to
  pursue writing full-time — a deliberate, self-directed career reversal
  away from a stable government engineering post and its income, taken
  before he had any track record as a published author. [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO]
- In 1844 he also anonymously published a translation of Balzac's
  *Eugénie Grandet* — his first concrete literary output, done as
  translation work rather than original composition, right at the point of
  leaving the military. [ENCYC]

## Life Period 2 — Early literary fame, ideological radicalization, arrest, and mock execution (1844–1849)

- Wrote his first novel, *Poor Folk*, over roughly nine months starting
  1844; on completion, editor Nikolai Nekrasov reportedly stayed up all
  night reading the manuscript and brought it to critic Vissarion
  Belinsky, declaring "a new Gogol has arisen." Belinsky himself then
  read it through the night and, on meeting Dostoevsky, called him
  Russia's first great "social novelist." [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO] [SEARCH-AGG]
- On this sudden acclaim (age 24), Dostoevsky wrote to his brother Mikhail
  in open elation: "I have a brilliant future before me, brother!" —
  direct primary-source evidence of his emotional reaction to a major
  professional triumph. [SEARCH-AGG]
- The acclaim was short-lived: his very next work, the psychologically
  experimental novella *The Double* (1846), was received coolly, including
  by Belinsky himself, who had championed him only months before. Belinsky
  turned critical of Dostoevsky's move away from straightforward social
  realism, and continued to criticize him in print until Belinsky's own
  death in 1848 — Dostoevsky's fame "cooled" essentially as fast as it had
  risen. [ENCYC] [SEARCH-AGG]
- Between 1846–1849, rather than retreating to a safer, previously
  successful mode after the *Double* setback, Dostoevsky experimented
  restlessly across very different literary forms and genres in rapid
  succession: social tales (*Mr. Prokharchin*, 1846), Gothic fiction
  (*The Landlady*, 1847), sentimental romance (*White Nights*, 1848), and
  an unfinished long work (*Netochka Nezvanova*) — evidence of persistence
  through critical rejection via continued output and formal
  experimentation rather than withdrawal. [ENCYC]
- In 1847 he joined the Petrashevsky Circle, a St. Petersburg discussion
  group of intellectuals engaging with utopian-socialist ideas (including
  Fourier), critical of serfdom and autocracy — a voluntary affiliation
  with a politically dangerous group under an autocratic regime known to
  suppress dissent. [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO]
- Arrested April 23, 1849, along with other Circle members; imprisoned for
  eight months (in the Peter-and-Paul Fortress per [FRANK]) before trial.
  Tried and, on November 16, 1849, condemned to death by firing squad
  along with 21 others. [ENCYC] [FRANK] [UBC-WIKI]
- December 22, 1849: taken to Semyonovsky Square. The condemned men were
  forced to kneel and kiss a cross, had their military ranks formally
  stripped, and underwent a ritual "breaking of swords" over their heads
  as a symbolic degradation/death rite. They were then brought out in
  groups of three, tied to posts, and blindfolded in preparation for
  actual shooting. Dostoevsky stood in the second group of three (with
  poets Aleksey Pleshcheyev and Sergey Durov). At the last possible moment
  — with rifles raised — a messenger arrived waving a white flag,
  announcing the Tsar's "pardon." Unknown to the prisoners at the time,
  Nicholas I had already decided on the commutation the day before; the
  entire near-execution was staged deliberately as psychological terror,
  not a last-second reprieve. [UBC-WIKI] [ENCYC]
- His documented immediate reaction, in a letter to his brother Mikhail
  written the same day, hours after the reprieve: "Why, didn't I face
  death for three-quarters of an hour today, live with this thought in my
  head, was I not a hairsbreadth away from death, and now I am living
  again!" He described feeling "reborn in another form," and later, in
  the same reflective vein, concluded "Life is a gift, life is happiness,
  each minute could be an eternity of bliss." This is direct primary
  correspondence describing his own psychological state in the immediate
  aftermath of extreme, life-threatening high-stakes pressure — a
  reaction of intensified will-to-live and religious/existential
  reframing rather than despair, paralysis, or anger at his captors.
  [UBC-WIKI, quoting the letter]
- His sentence was commuted to four years of hard labor in Siberia,
  followed by compulsory military service — not release. [ENCYC] [FRANK]

## Life Period 3 — Siberian hard labor, army service, first marriage (1850–1859)

- Served four years of hard labor at the Omsk fortress stockade (1850–54),
  living among ordinary criminal convicts (murderers, thieves), not
  political prisoners — a nobleman by birth thrown into daily close
  confinement with the peasant criminal class. [FRANK] [ENCYC]
- During this imprisonment he was permitted only a New Testament to read;
  this constrained access is specifically credited by biographers with
  catalyzing his embrace of Orthodox Christian faith during this period,
  a faith he retained and built on for the rest of his life. [ENCYC]
- He kept notes during imprisonment ("a collection of notes he had managed
  to take") recording "the unusual words and expressions of the peasant
  convicts, their arguments, their play-acting" — evidence of an active,
  ongoing habit of close observational note-taking even under the
  restrictive, punitive conditions of a labor camp. [SEARCH-AGG,
  Notes from a Dead House sourcing]
- As a former nobleman and political prisoner, he was reportedly despised
  and distrusted by most of the ordinary criminal convicts around him — he
  had to adapt to isolation/social hostility inside an already coercive
  environment, not just to the physical hardship of the labor itself.
  [SEARCH-AGG]
- He later wrote that he was astonished by the convicts' capacity to
  commit murders "without the slightest change in conscience," while also
  finding "good and decent individuals" blended among violent men — a
  documented instance of him actively revising simple/binary moral
  categories in light of direct field observation rather than retaining a
  prior abstract view. [SEARCH-AGG]
- After the four years' hard labor he served roughly six more years as an
  ordinary soldier in the army in Semipalatinsk (through 1859), remaining
  under the same broad sentence framework and away from literary
  St. Petersburg for a full decade total. [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO]
- He turned his prison experience into two minor novellas written while
  still in Siberia (neither critically successful at the time), and later
  into *Notes from the House of the Dead* (serialized 1860–1862), a
  thinly-fictionalized memoir using a nobleman narrator (Gorianchikov) who
  killed his wife, as a screening device — evidence of long-delayed,
  patient conversion of severe personal ordeal into disciplined literary
  work spanning years, rather than either suppressing the material or
  rushing it into print immediately upon release. [ENCYC] [SEARCH-AGG]
- In February 1857, while still in Siberian exile/service, he married
  Maria Dmitrievna Isaeva, a widow with a young son and no independent
  income. Multiple sources describe the marriage as troubled/unhappy, with
  the couple's difficulties (her tuberculosis, his epilepsy, both
  reportedly "reinforcing each other's unhealthy tendencies") beginning
  almost immediately. His epileptic seizures are described as
  intensifying dramatically in this post-release period. [ENCYC]
- He actively petitioned Tsar Alexander II for permission to return early
  from exile — an initiated, direct appeal to the same state authority
  that had condemned him — and was granted permission to return in
  December 1859, though he remained under secret police surveillance
  afterward. [ENCYC]

## Life Period 4 — Return to Petersburg, journal editing, brother's & wife's deaths, gambling begins (1859–1866)

- Returned to St. Petersburg and, together with brother Mikhail, founded
  and co-edited the literary/political monthly journal *Vremya* ("Time"),
  in which he published *Notes from the House of the Dead* and other work
  serially. This is direct evidence of him taking on an editorial/
  publishing-leadership role (not just being an author), managing
  deadlines, censorship pressure, and business operations jointly with his
  brother. [ENCYC] [SEARCH-AGG]
- *Vremya* was suppressed by the Russian authorities during this period —
  an external political/institutional setback to a venture he had
  personally built. [SEARCH-AGG] He and Mikhail later founded a successor
  journal, *Epokha* ("Epoch"). [YALE-BIO]
- During roughly 1861–1863 he pursued a difficult, emotionally
  destabilizing affair with Polina Suslova, a young student independently
  described as of "complicated and difficult temperament" — evidence of
  personal volatility and entanglement alongside his professional
  editorial responsibilities. [ENCYC]
- Traveled abroad to Western Europe in 1862–1863, explicitly to escape
  creditors, attend to his health, and indulge gambling — i.e., the
  European gambling pattern predates his second marriage and his most
  famous gambling crisis; it began during his first marriage. His written
  impressions of Europe from this trip, *Winter Notes on Summer
  Impressions* (1863), are described as sharply critical of Western
  European civilization, which he characterized as dominated by
  rationalism and "rapacious individualism" — an early, explicit written
  statement of the anti-Western-liberal position that hardens across his
  later career. [ENCYC]
- 1864: his first wife Maria died of tuberculosis; his brother and closest
  early literary collaborator Mikhail died only months later the same
  year. Two major personal losses in rapid succession. [ENCYC]
- On Mikhail's death, Dostoevsky discovered his brother had left the
  journal business roughly 20,000–25,000 rubles in debt, and Dostoevsky
  voluntarily assumed personal financial responsibility for his
  brother's widow and children (rather than disclaiming the debt), on top
  of already supporting his own adult stepson (Maria's son from her first
  marriage) and periodically assisting his younger brother Nikolai. He
  borrowed 10,000 rubles from a wealthy aunt to try to cover this, and by
  1865 was forced to formally declare bankruptcy (owing roughly 16,000
  rubles on notes plus 5,000 more on his word alone). This is a sustained,
  multi-year pattern of accepting family financial obligation well beyond
  what was legally required of him, at direct cost to his own solvency.
  [SEARCH-AGG]
- In the same period (1864) he wrote and published the philosophical
  novella *Notes from Underground*, developing the argument that people
  "desire freedom more than happiness" and will reject even a rationally
  optimal, imposed social order to preserve the freedom to choose badly —
  a fully worked-out anti-rationalist/anti-utopian position, written
  immediately alongside his most acute personal financial and emotional
  crisis. [ENCYC]

## Life Period 5 — Second marriage to Anna, the Stellovsky deadline, years abroad, and the height of the gambling addiction (1866–1871)

- 1866: facing a punitive publishing contract with Fyodor Stellovsky —
  deliver a wholly new novel by November 1, 1866, or forfeit all
  publishing rights to his existing and future work for nine years —
  Dostoevsky, on a friend's suggestion, hired a young stenographer, Anna
  Grigoryevna Snitkina, and dictated an entirely new novel, *The Gambler*,
  to her. The manuscript was completed in 26 days. When Stellovsky's
  office turned out to be closed (the publisher having left town), Anna
  reportedly suggested filing the manuscript with a notary instead —
  which they did, roughly two hours before the deadline, and courts later
  upheld this filing as valid. This episode shows extreme, sustained
  output under a hard external deadline with severe contractual
  consequences for failure, plus a documented instance of accepting and
  acting on a practical suggestion from a collaborator under pressure
  rather than insisting on his own plan alone. [SEARCH-AGG] [ENCYC]
- Dostoevsky married Anna Snitkina in February 1867; she was
  approximately 25 years his junior. [ENCYC] [ANNA-DIARY-context]
- To escape creditors, the newly married couple left Russia in 1867 and
  remained abroad (Germany, Switzerland, Italy) for over four years in
  straitened financial circumstances. [ENCYC]
- Anna's 1867 shorthand diary records the texture of their daily married
  life in this period: on May 2, 1867 she describes a day of
  "misunderstandings" and quarreling, writing "I was thankful when this
  miserable day came to an end, for I detest quarrelling," and noting his
  withholding of their usual goodnight kiss as an ominous sign; she also
  records an episode where, after restaurant staff treated them rudely,
  "Fyodor got into a terrible rage" and "completely lost his temper" —
  yet moments later pursued her with concern when she walked out, and the
  same diary records reconciliations after conflict, with him coming to
  bed and saying "a multitude of nice things." Anna also records her own
  fits of jealousy (e.g. imagining he was meeting another woman on an
  errand to a chemist's shop) which she treats candidly as her own
  disposition rather than something he provoked. This is direct, dated,
  first-person eyewitness evidence of volatile but quickly-resolving
  emotional expression within the marriage, from the person who observed
  it daily. [ANNA-DIARY]
- During this same period abroad, his gambling addiction reached its most
  acute and destructive phase. Direct-quoted letters to Anna from the
  gambling towns (Wiesbaden, Baden-Baden) show a repeating cycle: e.g.
  "My dear angel, lost again and badly. I sat at the table, and within
  half an hour all my money was gone," followed by "Forgive me for
  ruining your life." Anna, pregnant at the time, repeatedly pawned her
  own wedding ring, earrings, brooch, and a spare dress to fund him or to
  cover basic costs after his losses; she described one resulting episode
  of "dreadful convulsive sort of sobbing" on her own part. The pattern
  documented across multiple letters is confession → plea for forgiveness
  → promise never to repeat it → recurrence. [SEARCH-AGG]
- The gambling addiction had a documented hard end: April 24, 1871, after
  a final loss of his "last Taler" at the Wiesbaden casino, is recorded as
  the last time he ever gambled — i.e., a specific, dated, apparently
  durable behavior change after roughly a decade of repeated relapse,
  rather than a gradual fade. [SEARCH-AGG]
- *Crime and Punishment* (1866, i.e., written and published even before the
  Stellovsky/Gambler crisis fully resolved) became his most popular and
  commercially successful novel to date, centered on protagonist
  Raskolnikov's self-justifying "extraordinary man" theory for murder and
  his eventual confession — Dostoevsky was therefore sustaining major,
  demanding long-form creative work in parallel with acute personal
  financial/addiction crisis, not sequentially. [ENCYC]
- *The Idiot* (1868–69) and *The Possessed*/*Demons* (1871–72) were also
  written during this abroad period. *The Possessed* was directly inspired
  by and closely modeled on a real contemporary event — the 1869 murder
  of a student by the radical revolutionary Sergei Nechaev's circle — and
  used the novel to satirize both liberalism and revolutionary radicalism
  as interconnected dangers, a direct literary rebuttal of the same
  utopian-socialist milieu he himself had briefly belonged to twenty years
  earlier via the Petrashevsky Circle. [ENCYC]
- Anna is independently and repeatedly credited (by [RUSSIANLIFE] and by
  the general biographical consensus in [ENCYC]/[YALE-BIO]) as the
  stabilizing force that reversed the family's financial trajectory during
  and after this period — she took over management of publishing
  negotiations, contracts, and pricing on his behalf.

## Life Period 6 — Financial stability, mature novels, public influence, and death (1871–1881)

- After returning to Russia, Anna Dostoevskaya built and ran what is
  described as the first solo female-owned publishing house in Russia,
  managing dictation, editing, purchasing, and price negotiation
  independently; she reportedly visited competing publishers posing as a
  customer to gather competitive intelligence, and eventually published
  seven editions of Dostoevsky's collected works, a business template
  other authors' families (e.g. Sofia Tolstoy) later sought her advice on
  replicating. Dostoevsky is described as trusting her literary judgment
  implicitly — adjusting character or scene details in his manuscripts
  based on her feedback (his stated reasoning being that if she could
  clearly visualize a scene, he trusted a reader would too). By 1876 he
  wrote to her: "everything is in your power alone... I so highly value
  and trust in your mind." This documents a long-run shift toward
  delegating real authority (financial and, to a degree, editorial) to a
  trusted partner, after roughly a decade of exactly the opposite pattern
  (financial chaos he alone drove). [RUSSIANLIFE]
- The family purchased a house in the town of Staraya Russa during this
  period, their first real stable domestic base after the years of debt
  and travel. [YALE-BIO]
- In 1876 Dostoevsky began *A Writer's Diary*, first as a column and then,
  from 1876, as an independent monthly periodical he wrote, edited, and
  published essentially alone (fiction, current-events commentary,
  reporting on real sensational crimes, historical speculation, portraits
  of contemporaries, autobiographical pieces) — he suspended work on
  *The Brothers Karamazov* to sustain it. Readers wrote him letters in
  direct response to specific pieces, and he frequently replied to them
  within the publication itself, engaging in sustained direct
  correspondence with a lay public audience rather than only with critics
  or fellow writers. [SEARCH-AGG]
- His novella *The Meek One* (published within *A Writer's Diary*), about
  a young pawnbroker's wife's suicide, was directly inspired by a real
  newspaper account of a seamstress's suicide; he described the story's
  motivating idea as "a meek suicide, as if there were no forces left to
  continue living" — another example (as with *The Possessed*) of
  building major fiction directly and quickly off a real contemporary news
  event. [YALE-BIO]
- His long-running personal rivalry/feud with fellow novelist Ivan
  Turgenev, rooted in an explicit ideological split (Turgenev a Westernizing
  liberal, Dostoevsky by this period a conservative Slavophile nationalist)
  ran for years and included public mockery on both sides: Turgenev
  reportedly wrote a mocking poem calling Dostoevsky a "pimple on the nose
  of literature," while Dostoevsky, in a private 1867 letter, called
  liberals' criticism of Russia their "principal pleasure" and sarcastically
  told Turgenev to "buy a telescope" since he apparently could not see
  Russia at all (a jab at Turgenev's long expatriate residence in Western
  Europe). Notably, this ideological hostility coexisted with real
  financial dependence: after a gambling loss in Wiesbaden, Dostoevsky
  asked Turgenev directly for a loan; Turgenev sent only half of what was
  requested, and Dostoevsky did not repay it for eleven years — evidence
  he was willing to ask a rival for direct financial help under pressure,
  and (eventually) to make good on the resulting debt despite the ongoing
  personal antagonism. [SEARCH-AGG]
- *The Brothers Karamazov* was serialized 1879–1880, his final and most
  ambitious novel, centered on a patricide and four brothers each said to
  embody a different principle (passion, reason, faith, depravity/cynicism)
  — directly echoing his own family history of a father's disputed,
  possibly violent death forty years earlier. He sent the final installment
  (epilogue) to his publisher on November 8, 1880. [ENCYC]
- On June 8, 1880 (O.S.), he delivered a public speech at the unveiling of
  the Pushkin monument in Moscow, following a two-hour religious service.
  The speech praised Pushkin as the embodiment of Russia's national
  identity and is described as provoking something close to mass
  hysteria in the audience — people reportedly moved to tears, repeated
  bursts of applause — and, according to some accounts, briefly and
  unusually reconciled the rival Slavophile and Westernizer intellectual
  factions in the room, though other sources note it was initially
  rejected by some radical younger listeners. This is a documented instance
  of him exercising direct, large-scale public rhetorical influence,
  late in life, on the two ideological "sides" he had spent decades
  positioned against each other on. [SEARCH-AGG]
- Died January 28/February 9, 1881 (O.S./N.S.) in St. Petersburg, from
  complications related to a pulmonary hemorrhage, with his lifelong
  epilepsy noted by several sources as a contributing/associated factor to
  his declining health; he was buried at the Alexander Nevsky Monastery.
  [ENCYC] [YALE-BIO]
- Anna Dostoevskaya devoted the remainder of her own life (she outlived
  him by decades) to preserving and publishing his literary legacy; his
  final apartment later became a dedicated museum. [YALE-BIO]

## Cross-cutting observations (not new episodes — pattern notes across the periods above)

- **Working habits / creative process**: repeatedly documented pattern of
  producing major, complex work under acute external deadline pressure
  (the *Gambler* 26-day sprint; multiple serialized novels written and
  delivered in monthly installments to journals he sometimes also edited),
  and of converting his own direct, often traumatic lived experience
  (the mock execution, the Siberian prison years, his father's disputed
  death, real contemporary news stories) into fiction, sometimes after long
  delay (Siberia → *House of the Dead*, published years later) and
  sometimes almost immediately (a newspaper suicide → *The Meek One*
  within the same publication cycle).
- **Financial behavior**: a genuinely two-part, sequential pattern — roughly
  fifteen years (early 1860s–1871) of chronic self-generated financial
  crisis (gambling, bad publishing contracts, voluntarily assumed family
  debt) followed by a documented, dated behavioral turning point (quitting
  gambling for good in April 1871) and then roughly a decade of relative
  financial stability, substantially attributable by multiple sources to
  ceding real financial/business authority to Anna.
- **Ideological trajectory**: a clear, well-documented reversal from
  youthful sympathy with utopian-socialist/Fourierist ideas (Petrashevsky
  Circle, pre-1849) to a mature conservative, Orthodox-Christian,
  Slavophile-nationalist position hostile to Western liberalism and
  radicalism (*Winter Notes*, *The Possessed*, the Turgenev feud, the
  Pushkin speech) — with the Siberian imprisonment/exile period
  consistently identified across sources as the causal hinge.

### Key achievements
1. **Survived a staged mock execution and used the experience as a
   permanent creative and philosophical touchstone**, from *The Idiot*
   to his private correspondence, rather than merely surviving it.
   [UBC-WIKI]
2. **Wrote *Crime and Punishment*, *The Idiot*, *Demons*, and *The
   Brothers Karamazov* — four novels now considered foundational to
   world literature — while simultaneously managing acute, sustained
   personal financial and addiction crises**, not in some calmer,
   separate period of his life. [ENCYC]
3. **Turned severe personal ordeal (Siberian imprisonment, his father's
   disputed death, a mock execution) directly into disciplined,
   internationally influential literary work**, sometimes only after
   years of delay. [ENCYC, UBC-WIKI]
4. **Delivered the 1880 Pushkin Monument speech**, a rare documented
   instance of a Russian writer briefly and directly moving a divided
   national audience through live public rhetoric. [SEARCH-AGG]

### Revealing anecdotes
1. **"I have a brilliant future before me, brother!"** — his own
   elated letter on first literary acclaim at 24, before that fame
   collapsed within the year. [SEARCH-AGG]
2. **The mock execution and its letter.** Minutes after believing he
   was about to be shot, he wrote to his brother that he felt "reborn"
   and that "life is a gift" — a direct, dated, first-person account of
   extreme psychological reframing under lethal threat. [UBC-WIKI]
3. **The 26-day sprint that produced *The Gambler*.** Facing forfeiture
   of all future publishing rights, he dictated an entire novel to a
   young stenographer (his future wife, Anna) and filed it with a
   notary roughly two hours before the deadline. [SEARCH-AGG]
4. **Quitting gambling for good.** After roughly a decade of relapse,
   his very last loss at Wiesbaden on April 24, 1871 is the last time
   he is recorded ever gambling again — an abrupt, durable, and dated
   behavior change. [SEARCH-AGG]

### Turning points
1. **The 1849 mock execution and Siberian exile** — the single event
   most consistently identified across sources as the hinge between his
   youthful radicalism and his mature conservative, religious
   worldview. [ENCYC, UBC-WIKI]
2. **His brother Mikhail's death (1864) and the resulting debt** —
   forced him into a decade of financial crisis and gambling, but also
   into the second marriage (to Anna) that eventually stabilized his
   life. [SEARCH-AGG]
3. **April 1871: quitting gambling for good**, closing a roughly
   decade-long destructive cycle and opening his most stable and
   productive final decade. [SEARCH-AGG]
